                                   Introduction
                                   Artificial intelligence is becoming an integral component of contemporary medical education.1,2 Large language models
                                   (LLMs) such as GPT-4 have attracted growing attention for their capacity to synthesize medical knowledge, produce
                                   structured clinical responses, and emulate aspects of clinical reasoning within digitally supported learning environments.
                                   Beyond LLMs specifically, medical trainees increasingly rely on diverse digital resources for clinical skills learning,
                                   including video-based training materials and generative AI tools, reflecting evolving self-directed learning behaviors
                                   among undergraduate and postgraduate trainees.3–5
                                       The capabilities of LLMs have been explored through their performance on standardized medical examinations.
                                   Several publications have evaluated LLM performance on United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE)-style
                                   preparation questions and board-style practice materials, with GPT-4 demonstrating performance comparable to or
                                   exceeding that of senior medical students in some reports, though it should be noted that the majority of these studies
                                   used test preparation questions rather than authentic examination forms.6,7 Studies from Japan have shown that LLMs
                                   can perform competitively on national medical licensing questions, although performance declined on more context-
                                   dependent clinical tasks.8–11 These findings suggest LLMs may serve as credible tools for medical learning and formative
                                   assessment.12,13
                                       Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) represent a cornerstone of clinical skills assessment in under­
                                   graduate medical training, providing a structured approach to evaluating students’ performance across multiple compe­
                                   tency domains.14,15 Despite their recognized validity and standardization, OSCE design and implementation involve
                                   significant demands in terms of faculty time, standardized patient training, and logistical organization.16,17 Ensuring the
                                   quality and reliability of OSCE scoring instruments, including analytic checklist design, item weighting, and inter-rater
                                   consistency, remains a recognized methodological challenge in clinical skills assessment.18,19
                                       Although several studies have explored LLMs in OSCE-like contexts, the evidence base remains limited and
                                   heterogeneous, and few investigations have used validated analytic rubrics from a nationally standardized OSCE
                                   program.20–22 Prior work has largely focused on comparing candidate-level performance, whereas the potential use of
                                   LLM-generated responses to audit OSCE checklists for completeness, internal consistency, and alignment with official
                                   learning objectives has been insufficiently explored.23
                                       We hypothesized that an LLM could contribute to OSCE-based education by acting as a simulated candidate,
                                   generating structured responses to OSCE scenarios systematically comparable against validated assessment rubrics.
                                   Rather than evaluating learner performance, this approach aims to explore the potential of LLMs to support the pre-

                                   This work is intended primarily to inform medical educators and OSCE station developers responsible for checklist
                                   design and quality assurance, rather than to serve as a trainee-facing exam-preparation tool; the prompting strategy
                                   therefore reflects a rigorous, reference-informed simulated response rather than the prompting an individual trainee might
                                   typically use.
                                       The objective of this proof-of-concept study was to evaluate whether an advanced LLM can (1) generate structured,
                                   rubric-mappable responses to validated OSCE stations and (2) provide preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence,
                                   obtained through human-adjudicated comparison of LLM outputs with official reference documents, on whether such an
                                   approach could eventually support OSCE station quality assurance by helping surface missing, ambiguous, or incon­
                                   sistent elements within analytic scoring grids for subsequent expert review.


                                   Materials and Methods
                                   Ethical Considerations
                                   This study involved no patients, no student performance data, and no identifiable personal information. It used de-
                                   identified educational materials (validated station scenarios and scoring rubrics) and LLM-generated text outputs. In
                                   accordance with French regulations for research in medical education without human participants, the project was
                                   considered exempt from formal ethics committee review. It was authorized by the faculty pedagogical board and
                                   registered under IRB number IRB00014528_2025_26. The study complied with GDPR principles.


                                   National OSCE Framework and Competency-Based Design
                                   In France, OSCEs (ECOS) are nationally standardized and embedded in a competency-based curriculum. Stations are
                                   derived from nationally defined “Starting Clinical Situations” (SCS) and aligned with LiSA learning items, LiSA being
                                   the officially endorsed national digital reference platform for French undergraduate medical education, providing
                                   standardized learning objectives (“items”) and content validated by national disciplinary colleges for each subject,
                                   specifying expected undergraduate outcomes. Each station is designed to assess a primary competency domain and
                                   may target a secondary domain. Clinical competencies are organized into 11 nationally defined domains encompassing
                                   communication, history taking, clinical examination, procedures, diagnostic reasoning, therapeutic decision-making, and
                                   emergency management.


                                   Station Selection
                                   We selected 10 validated OSCE stations used in high-stakes examinations for final-year medical students (6th year) at the
                                   Faculty of Medicine of Nice, France, designed to cover all 11 nationally defined competency domains across primary and
                                   secondary domains (Table 1). Stations included procedural, medical, pediatric, geriatric, and communication-focused
                                   scenarios, and used supporting documents when applicable (eg, laboratory results or clinical images).


                                   LLM Configuration and Prompting
                                   LLM-assisted analyses were performed using GPT-4 Turbo (OpenAI; model identifier gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09), accessed
                                   through the ChatGPT Plus consumer subscription interface (not the developer API) in May 2025. A fixed system prompt
                                   was used identically across all stations and both conditions (full prompt text provided in Supplementary Material S1).
                                   The prompt instructed the model to respond as a final-year French medical student (DFASM3) sitting an OSCE station,
                                   with explicit instructions to produce structured, clinically relevant responses aligned with national undergraduate
                                   standards, to prioritize patient safety and mandatory clinical actions, and to avoid meta-commentary or AI self-
                                   identification. Browsing, code interpreter, and other plugin tools were manually disabled in the chat settings to
                                   standardize outputs. Because access occurred through the consumer interface rather than a version-pinned API endpoint,
                                   generation parameters such as temperature and top-p were not user-configurable and reflect the platform’s undisclosed
                                   default settings for this model at the time of access; this constitutes an additional reproducibility caveat discussed further
                                   in the Limitations. Each new station session was initiated independently, with no persistent memory of previous stations.

                                   Each station was run once in the baseline condition and once in the reference-augmented (RAG) condition. All station
                                   runs were conducted within a single, contiguous data-collection window in May 2025 to minimize the risk of model
                                   version drift across stations; nonetheless, because consumer-facing LLM interfaces may be updated by the provider
                                   without notice, silent backend changes during this window cannot be fully excluded.
                                       This single-run, single-station design was chosen for this initial proof-of-concept exploration. As a result, output
                                   variability across runs was not assessed, and findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating
                                   rather than as reliable performance estimates. Future studies should include multiple repeated runs per station to quantify
                                   intra-station variance and assess output stability.

                                   Inputs and Outputs
                                   For each station, the model received the same written material available to students at the beginning of the station
                                   (scenario instructions and supporting documents). In the RAG condition, the model additionally received the official
                                   textual content of the associated SCS and LiSA reference documents. The model was instructed to provide a structured
                                   response aligned with OSCE expectations (eg, targeted history questions, examination steps, diagnostic reasoning, and
                                   management plan, depending on the station).

                                   Scoring and Outcome Definitions
                                   All LLM outputs were scored against the official analytic station rubric by two clinical educators (G.M. and E.L.)
                                   working independently through human review, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Inter-rater agreement between
                                   the two reviewers prior to consensus resolution was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.84 for checklist item scoring). Each
                                   checklist item was coded as achieved or not achieved based on explicit presence in the model output; partial, implicit, or
                                   paraphrased responses were credited as achieved only when a reviewer judged the clinical intent and content to be
                                   unambiguously equivalent to the rubric item, and were otherwise coded as not achieved. This binary approach, consistent
                                   with the national OSCE rubric format, does not capture gradations of quality, sequencing, or communication proficiency,
                                   a limitation discussed further below. The primary outcome was checklist coverage, defined as the percentage of rubric
                                   items achieved out of the total number of items for that station. A-level items were defined as mandatory, safety-critical
                                   items in the national rubric. The A-level omission rate was defined as the percentage of total checklist items that were A-
                                   level and not achieved. For the RAG condition, we report (1) checklist coverage under reference augmentation and (2)
                                   the proportion of previously missed A- or B-level items that were recovered; a formal stratified breakdown of recovered
                                   items by A- versus B-level was not performed in this proof-of-concept dataset and is noted as a limitation below.

                                   Off-Checklist Content
                                   All clinically oriented statements not represented in the rubric checklist were extracted and classified as: (i) clinically
                                   relevant and consistent with national references, (ii) acceptable but not required, or (iii) incorrect/irrelevant (hallucina­
                                   tions). Off-checklist statements were identified through line-by-line comparison of each LLM output against the
                                   corresponding station rubric, independent of the primary checklist scoring pass. Two clinical educators (G.M. and E.
                                   L.) adjudicated these statements; disagreements were resolved by consensus. Because the LLM produced text-only
                                   outputs without embodied interaction, attitude grids, non-verbal behavior, procedural execution quality, and examiner
                                   global ratings were not scored.

                                   Statistical Analysis
                                   Analyses were performed using JASP software (version 0.19.3; University of Amsterdam). Continuous variables were
                                   expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Paired comparisons between baseline and RAG coverage scores were performed
                                   using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, given the paired design, small sample size (n=10), and non-normal distribution of
                                   percentage data. Effect size was quantified using the rank-biserial correlation (95% CI for the mean baseline-to-RAG
                                   difference: approximately 7.7 to 20.4 percentage points). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically
                                   significant. Because the 10 stations were purposively selected (one per competency domain) rather than randomly
                                   sampled, and station-level observations may be correlated through shared assessment format, inferential statistics should

                                   Results
                                   LLM performance across the 10 evaluated stations is summarized in Table 2 and Figure 1. Mean baseline checklist
                                   coverage was 81% (SD 10.2), ranging from 67% to 93%. The model omitted on average 10% (SD 7.2) of A-level items,
                                   with a maximum omission of 23% in a single station. Reported standard deviations reflect between-station variability
                                   under a single-run design and do not capture within-station run-to-run variability, which could not be estimated in this
                                   proof-of-concept study (see Limitations). No comparator dataset of human candidate performance on these same stations
                                   was available; the educational significance of the observed coverage values relative to human performance therefore
                                   cannot be directly benchmarked and should be interpreted with this caveat.
                                       In the RAG condition (LiSA and SCS reference text additionally provided), mean checklist coverage increased to
                                   95% (SD 5.1), corresponding to a mean increase of 15.4 percentage points (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.002; rank-
                                   biserial correlation = 0.91; 95% CI for mean difference, approximately 7.7–20.4 percentage points). This corresponded to
                                   recovery of 1–4 previously missed checklist items per station. Among remaining missed items after RAG, three
                                   categories of failure were identified: (i) rubric items not clearly supported by the provided reference documents, (ii)
                                   items requiring implicit clinical inference not reliably prioritized by the model, and (iii) rubric-scenario inconsistencies in
                                   which the clinical vignette did not supply sufficient information to justify the expected rubric answer.
                                       The LLM generated off-checklist additions in every station, averaging 22% (SD 6.7) of checklist items per station.
                                   Nearly all were judged clinically relevant or acceptable extensions of the expected response. One incorrect addition was
                                   identified in the hearing loss/deafness station.
                                       Checklist coverage tended to be higher in stations emphasizing structured, action-oriented tasks (clinical examination,
                                   procedure, iconography, treatment strategy; mean 91.6% ± 4.1) than in stations requiring dialogue-heavy interaction

                                   Discussion
                                   This proof-of-concept study evaluated an LLM as a simulated candidate in validated French national OSCE stations,
                                   using official analytic rubrics as the evaluation framework, and explored a complementary use case: rubric auditing for
                                   station pre-validation and quality assurance. The results suggest that an LLM can generate structured responses that map
                                   to checklist items, while also highlighting areas where rubric elements may be missing, ambiguous, or inconsistently
                                   supported by the station vignette and reference documents. We wish to explicitly clarify the scope of this proof-of-
                                   concept: our analyses evaluate the LLM primarily as a simulated OSCE candidate against existing, validated rubrics; they
                                   do not constitute a formal method for checklist pre-validation or quality assurance, which would require prospective
                                   evaluation of the LLM’s ability to review and improve rubrics not yet finalized. The rubric-related observations reported
                                   here (missing, ambiguous, or inconsistent items surfaced through human-adjudicated comparison of LLM outputs with
                                   rubrics) should be regarded as a promising, hypothesis-generating signal for this future application rather than as direct
                                   evidence of an effective LLM-assisted checklist QA method.
                                       Most previous investigations of LLMs in medical education have focused on performance in knowledge-based
                                   written examinations, primarily assessing factual knowledge and clinical reasoning in static formats.1,2,6,7 In contrast,
                                   OSCEs are designed to assess performance-based competencies using standardized clinical scenarios and structured
                                   scoring tools, with a strong emphasis on content validity and alignment between learning objectives and assessment
                                   criteria.14,24 This theoretical alignment can be situated within Miller’s pyramid of clinical competence, in which OSCEs

                                       The heterogeneous performance across competency domains is consistent with the intrinsic nature of OSCE
                                   competency domains: technically structured and decision-oriented competencies are more easily operationalized through
                                   textual responses, whereas relational and interaction-based competencies depend on dynamic exchanges, contextual
                                   adaptation, and non-verbal communication. This limitation may partly reflect the unidirectional evaluation framework
                                   used in the present study. In an interactive interview-based OSCE setting with iterative question-answer exchanges,
                                   performance in relational domains could potentially improve.
                                       A further contribution of this study is that LLM-generated responses, when systematically compared with analytic
                                   rubrics by human reviewers, may help surface missing, ambiguous, or inconsistent items within OSCE scoring grids. It is
                                   important to note that the LLM itself did not independently or validly audit the rubrics: in the present design, the model
                                   generated candidate-like responses, and trained human reviewers subsequently interpreted off-checklist or unmatched
                                   content as potentially relevant to rubric completeness. The study therefore demonstrates that LLM outputs may serve as
                                   prompts for human-led rubric reflection, rather than that LLMs can autonomously audit OSCE rubrics. This distinction is
                                   particularly relevant in nationally standardized frameworks, where any use of LLM outputs to inform checklist revisions
                                   should be validated by an independent expert panel before implementation, to avoid introducing new errors or biases into
                                   examination content.
                                       Off-checklist additions should be interpreted with precision. In AI safety terminology, “hallucination” refers to
                                   incorrect or unfounded content. In contrast, many additions observed here were clinically appropriate extensions simply
                                   absent from the scoring grid. This distinction matters for educators: clinically correct off-checklist content may indicate
                                   that the rubric is incomplete or overly rigid, whereas incorrect additions represent genuine safety risks. Although the
                                   incorrect-addition rate was low in this dataset, broader risks inherent to LLM deployment in this context, including model
                                   drift across silent provider-side updates, algorithmic or training-data bias, and residual hallucination risk, should be
                                   weighed carefully before any operational use, and warrant dedicated monitoring rather than one-off evaluation.
                                       Several limitations must be acknowledged. Although mean overall checklist coverage was high, the mean A-level
                                   omission rate of 10% (up to 23% in one station) warrants particular attention: A-level items denote mandatory, safety-
                                   critical actions in the national rubric, and even modest omission rates at this level may carry disproportionate educational
                                   and patient-safety significance not captured by aggregate coverage percentages alone. First and most critically, each
                                   station was run only once per condition. Given the known stochastic variability of LLM outputs, a single run cannot
                                   determine whether observed checklist item omissions or RAG-driven recoveries are systematic or incidental. Future
                                   studies must include multiple repeated runs per station to quantify intra-station variance and assess output stability.
                                   Second, with only 10 stations and one to two stations per competency domain, domain-level comparisons are purely
                                   exploratory and cannot support generalizable conclusions. Third, LLM outputs are text-only and cannot represent
                                   embodied procedural performance, real-time interaction, non-verbal communication, or examiner global judgments.
                                   Fourth, the possibility that the LLM’s training data included similar OSCE materials cannot be excluded, and may
                                   have partially inflated baseline checklist coverage. Fifth, no human student comparator cohort was included, limiting
                                   interpretability of absolute scores. Sixth, although a fixed prompt was used, the model was accessed through a consumer
                                   interface rather than a version-pinned API, so generation parameters (including temperature) were not user-configurable
                                   and exact reproducibility of outputs cannot be guaranteed. Seventh, the fixed system prompt referred to the provision of

                                   eliminate this ambiguity. Eighth, this study was conducted using a single LLM, in a single language, within a single

                                   assessment systems, or countries, and model-specific behavior or bias cannot be excluded as a contributor to the observed
                                   results. Ninth, the RAG recovery analysis was not stratified by A- versus B-level item, limiting insight into which
                                   recovered items were safety-critical.
                                       In summary, this exploratory study suggests that LLMs can operate as simulated OSCE candidates while offering
                                   a preliminary, hypothesis-generating signal regarding a novel mechanism for reviewing and strengthening assessment
                                   tools, a use case that will require dedicated prospective validation. Rather than replacing human examiners, LLMs may
                                   serve as complementary decision-support systems, subject to independent expert oversight. Substantial follow-up work
                                   with multiple LLM runs per station, larger station banks, formal reliability modeling, multiple models and languages, and
                                   a human comparator cohort is required before any implementation recommendations can be made. Practically, such
                                   workflows could support faculty review of newly drafted stations by flagging candidate items for discussion prior to
                                   examination deployment, complementing rather than replacing standard expert-panel checklist validation.


                                   Conclusion
                                   This proof-of-concept study shows that an LLM can generate structured, rubric-mappable OSCE responses that map onto
                                   official analytic rubrics, and that reference-augmented prompting substantially improves checklist coverage. Human-
                                   adjudicated comparison of LLM outputs with existing rubrics surfaced candidate elements of checklist ambiguity or
                                   incompleteness, suggesting a hypothesis-generating, exploratory role for LLMs as future decision-support tools in OSCE
                                   station pre-validation, pending replication with multiple runs, larger and more diverse station banks, formal reproduci­
                                   bility analysis, and prospective validation of any LLM-assisted rubric-review workflow by independent expert panels.
