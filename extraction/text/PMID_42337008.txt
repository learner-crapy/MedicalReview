Abstract


In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) is a critical ophthalmic examination that provides in vivo cytological

and neurological information essential for diagnosing corneal and certain systemic diseases, but its clinical

utility is limited by time-consuming interpretation and the need for subspecialty expertise. We developed

IVCM-Insight, an artificial intelligence (AI) system integrating image–text contrastive learning with large

language models (LLMs) for automated report generation and interactive question answering (QA). Based

on 30,368 IVCM images and 4,155 paired clinical reports, the model was trained with contrastive alignment,

image-conditioned language modeling, and multi-image consistency loss to produce structured diagnostic

reports while a domain-adapted LLM supported patient-centered QA. Automated evaluation showed strong
                                                                        S
agreement with the reference reports: Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU)-1 to BLEU-4 scores were
                                                                ES
0.69, 0.58, 0.47, and 0.41, Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation (ROUGE-L) was 0.67,
                                                         PR
Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation (CIDEr) was 1.85, and Metric for Evaluation of Translation

with Explicit ORdering (METEOR) was 0.66. In addition, the multi-label classification achieved an accuracy
                                                IN

Introduction


In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) is a non-invasive, high-resolution imaging technique that enables real-

time visualization of living tissues at the cellular resolution without biopsy 1–3. In ophthalmology, IVCM is

widely used to assess corneal microstructural features, including corneal deposits, subbasal nerves, and

inflammatory cell-related changes, etc. 4–7. In neuroscience, corneal nerve and immune-cell-associated

readouts from IVCM have been investigated as minimally invasive biomarkers in systemic and neurologic

diseases, including type 2 diabetes-related neuropathy and inflammation, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple

sclerosis 8–11. However, IVCM provides image-based data rather than direct neural information, making

interpretation time-consuming and highly expertise-dependent 12. In remote or densely populated settings,
                                                                          S
there is an urgent need for a reliable, generalizable AI-assisted system to support image interpretation.
                                                                  ES
Moreover, the technical language of IVCM reports often limits patient understanding, underscoring the
                                                           PR
necessity of AI-driven tools to aid both interpretation and report generation.


                                                  IN
Generative AI models create clinically relevant text and images by learning data patterns 13. Their use in

medicine has expanded rapidly, especially for image interpretation and automated reporting 14,15. Among
                                        LE
them, large language models (LLMs) generate human-like responses to complex queries 16 and, in
                                    C
ophthalmology, support imaging explanations, structured summaries, and patient-friendly communication
                              TI
    . Generative AI also enables fundus analysis, optical coherence tomography (OCT) reporting, and retinal
17–20
                          R
                      A
pathology characterization 21–23, underscoring its potential to improve diagnosis, efficiency, and patient care.


To our knowledge, no AI model has been specifically developed for the comprehensive interpretation and

automated report generation of IVCM images. Previous studies have primarily applied deep learning to

narrowly defined tasks, such as disease detection or classification. For example, Ruiz-Lozano et al. analyzed

subbasal nerve plexus features at the inferior whorl in neuropathic corneal pain and dry eye disease using

deep learning 24, while Shareef et al. 25 and Lincke et al. 26 developed AI-based systems for diagnosing

Acanthamoeba keratitis from IVCM images. While these approaches have demonstrated clinical value by

improving diagnostic accuracy, their scope remains limited, lacking the capacity to integrate diverse image

features, provide narrative explanations, or generate structured clinical reports that support broader decision-

making. Furthermore, they lack interactive capabilities, which prevents them from supporting

communication between clinicians and patients.
                                      ARTICLE IN PRESS




To overcome these limitations, we developed a framework that integrates contrastive learning with LLMs to

generate accurate IVCM reports and support diagnostic decisions, aiming to improve clinical efficiency and

broaden access to corneal imaging technologies. Furthermore, by embedding a patient-centered QA

component into this workflow, we build directly on the novel paradigm proposed by Badian et al. 27 to bridge

the gap between automated algorithm verification and contextual, person-centered clinical application..

The performance of IVCM-Insight in automated report generation was evaluated using standard NLG and

MLC metrics, demonstrating strong agreement with reference reports (Table 3). In the test dataset, the

automated report generation module of our deployed implementation took 2.68±0.61 s/case, substantially


less than manual reporting 209.9±41.5 s/case. The model achieved BLEU1-4 scores of 0.69, 0.58, 0.47, and
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




0.41; a ROUGE-L score of 0.67; a CIDEr score of 1.85; and a METEOR score of 0.66. These results indicate

strong lexical and semantic concordance with the original reports. MLC metrics demonstrated that IVCM-

Insight effectively identified key pathological conditions from IVCM reports, supporting image

interpretation and automated report generation. The system achieved an average accuracy of 0.96 and an F1

score of 0.80. At the condition level, performance was high with F1 scores of 0.95 for epithelial defects, 0.88

Inflammatory cell infiltration, affecting multiple layers, achieved the highest performance (F1 = 0.99).

Clinical Application of IVCM-Insight


To evaluate the clinical applicability of IVCM-Insight, the system was implemented in a series of selected

cases alongside routine clinical reporting. Each case was independently diagnosed and documented through

the standard clinical workflow, with IVCM-Insight applied exclusively as a supplementary tool to enable

comparative analysis. Representative cases illustrating its performance and potential clinical utility are

presented in Figure 4.


Across different conditions, IVCM-Insight reproduced key findings consistent with clinician-generated
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




reports. In cytomegalovirus keratitis (Figure 4A), the system identified keratic precipitates and owl’s eye

appearance cells, with the QA module explaining their association with chronic inflammation and endothelial

dysfunction. In fungal keratitis (Figure 4B), epithelial swelling, inflammatory infiltration, and stromal

hyphae extending to 132 μm were detected, with the QA module underscoring their diagnostic value for

confirming infection and estimating disease severity. In Acanthamoeba keratitis (Figure 4C), epithelial

defects, cyst-like structures, and stromal activation were recognized, and the QA module clarified that cysts

represent the resistant form of Acanthamoeba, highlighting their role in persistent infection and the

consequent need for targeted therapeutic intervention. Taken together, these representative cases demonstrate

the ability of IVCM-Insight to complement standard practice by generating structured reports and providing

patient-accessible explanations. While exploratory in nature, these findings indicate potential clinical value

and highlight the necessity of validation in larger, systematically assessed cohorts.

Discussion


IVCM is a critical ophthalmic examination that provides in vivo cytological and neurological information

essential for diagnosing corneal and certain systemic diseases 28. However, the interpretation of IVCM

images relies heavily on a limited number of subspecialty experts, which significantly restricts its routine

clinical application and widespread adoption 12. In this study, we developed IVCM-Insight, an AI system

that innovatively integrates contrastive learning with LLMs to facilitate automated IVCM report generation

                                                                          S
and interactive clinical question–answering. The system was validated using both automated metrics,
                                                                   ES
including NLG and MLC, as well as manual evaluation by experienced ophthalmologists, demonstrating

                                                             PR
high reliability and clinical relevance. These results highlight the potential of advanced AI approaches to

improve IVCM interpretation and communication, addressing a critical gap by providing a dedicated tool
                                                  IN
for IVCM report generation and interactive clinical explanation.

                                         LE
At present, most AI models based on IVCM remain focused on image-level deep learning. By constructing
                                     C
disease-specific datasets, these models have been applied to image classification to assist in corneal disease
                               TI
diagnosis 6,29,30, to differentiate inflammatory cell types 31, and other tasks. Essalat et al. employed multiple
                           R
CNN architectures to distinguish Acanthamoeba keratitis, fungal keratitis, nonspecific keratitis, and normal
                       A
corneas using IVCM images 29. Lin et al. developed a model to detect fungal hyphae in IVCM images with

an accuracy of 93.6% 30. Koseoglu et al. trained an EfficientNet-B3 on a large multicenter dataset to identify

microneuromas, enabling screening for neuropathic corneal pain 6. Beyond classification, annotation of

IVCM elements such as cell boundaries and nerves has enabled deep learning models to perform entity

recognition tasks, supporting quantitative analysis of corneal nerve morphology 32–35 and automated

endothelial cell counting 36–38. Additionally, Tang et al. proposed an unsupervised framework for anomaly

detection in IVCM images, aiming to identify atypical cellular or structural features without relying on

extensive labeled datasets 39. Recently, further progress has been made in IVCM imaging itself. Downie et

al. combined time-lapse imaging with computational methods to develop the FUN-IVCM software 40–43,

enabling repeated scanning of the same tissue region and, for the first time, in vivo characterization of corneal
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




cell motility, thereby expanding the clinical capabilities of IVCM. Collectively, these studies underscore the

progress in AI-driven image analysis for IVCM, while highlighting the need for systems that integrate

higher-level reasoning, report generation, and interactive clinical interpretation. Despite these advances,

existing approaches exhibit several common limitations. First, they are generally restricted to single-image

analysis and do not integrate information across multiple corneal layers or regions, limiting their applicability

to single-disease or lesion-specific tasks. Second, these models rely predominantly on image-level

supervision and do not incorporate the rich semantic information embedded in clinical reports, limiting their

ability to generate structured diagnostic outputs.


To address these gaps, we developed a structured report generation model within IVCM-Insight, based on
                                                                          S
an image–text contrastive learning framework. The model was trained on a comprehensive IVCM dataset
                                                                   ES
encompassing all corneal layers, scanning depths, and lesion regions, enabling the integration of multi-
                                                           PR
layered spatial information. By aligning visual features with textual descriptions, the model generated

coherent and clinically interpretable reports. Furthermore, through automated keyword extraction, IVCM-
                                                  IN
Insight accurately captured key pathological findings, demonstrating strong performance in detecting

                                         LE
common abnormalities such as epithelial defects, reduced subbasal nerve density, and inflammatory cell

                                     C
infiltration. This framework facilitates a more holistic analysis of corneal pathology while substantially
                               TI
reducing report preparation time and dependence on physician expertise.
                           R
                       A
In clinical practice, effective physician–patient communication is essential 44. However, the technical

complexity of IVCM reports often impedes patient comprehension. To our knowledge, no LLM has been

specifically designed to analyze and interpret IVCM reports. To address this gap, IVCM-Insight incorporates

an LLM-driven interactive QA module. This module generates concise, accessible, and accurate responses

to patient queries regarding report content and key findings, while ensuring clinical safety, thereby

facilitating more effective communication. Notably, this capability is particularly valuable in primary

healthcare settings, where limited clinical experience with IVCM may impede accurate interpretation,

despite the availability of automated structured reports. By harnessing the interpretive and explanatory power

of LLMs, clinicians can achieve a more comprehensive understanding of report content, better recognize the

pathological features captured by IVCM, and more effectively apply its clinical information. In this way,

IVCM-Insight has the potential to overcome the “last mile” barrier to IVCM adoption in community practice,
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




ultimately contribute to improving standards of corneal disease diagnosis and management.


Contrastive learning is a self-supervised learning paradigm that acquires discriminative and generalizable

feature representations by drawing semantically similar samples closer in the embedding space while

pushing dissimilar samples farther apart. Its application in ophthalmology has recently attracted increasing

attention. For example, Chen et al. 18 developed a contrastive learning framework based on the BLIP model

for fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) image–report pairs, which was further integrated with the

LLaMA-2 model to enable automated report generation and interactive question–answering. Similarly, Shi

et al. 45 leveraged a CLIP-based framework incorporating eleven ophthalmic imaging modalities and text

pairs, achieving strong performance in disease classification, visual question answering, and cross-modal
                                                                          S
retrieval tasks. Inspired by these advances, the contrastive learning module in this study was designed in a
                                                                  ES
BLIP-like fashion, employing a dual-stream encoder and cross-modal decoder to achieve precise image–text
                                                           PR
alignment. Furthermore, joint optimization through contrastive learning, image-conditioned language

modeling, and multi-image consistency constraints were applied to enhance semantic coherence across
                                                  IN
multiple corneal layers within the same patient. This design constitutes a methodological advance over

                                        LE
previous IVCM-based AI models, which were largely restricted to single-disease classification, by enabling

                                    C
the generation of structured, logically coherent, and clinically interpretable diagnostic reports.
                              TI
To further enhance system interactivity, this study also incorporated an LLM. LLMs are deep neural
                          R
                      A
networks trained on massive text corpora, capable of understanding and generating natural language, and

demonstrating human-like reasoning and expressive abilities across a wide range of tasks. In ophthalmology,

Wu et al. 46 explored the application of LLMs in responding to questions related to diabetic retinopathy (DR),

validating through expert evaluation that such models can provide educational and explanatory support for

patients. Building on this, Haghighi et al. 47 demonstrated that domain-specific fine-tuning with

ophthalmology-focused corpora can markedly improve the performance of LLM-based question–answering

systems. In our study, we constructed a domain-adapted corpus comprising 30 representative clinical

question–answer pairs and applied lightweight fine-tuning to DeepSeek:70B, a bilingual LLM with strong

ophthalmic text-generation capability. This strategy enabled the system to achieve a complete functional

loop in the IVCM setting, bridging automated report generation with patient-centered communication.


In this study, IVCM-Insight established a generalizable and efficient paradigm for medical image analysis
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




and interpretation. Beyond IVCM, this framework has the potential to be extended to other ophthalmic

modalities, including slit-lamp photography, OCT, FFA, and even histopathological analysis. More

importantly, the model demonstrates broad scalability and could be applied to systemic diseases, thereby

supporting cross-disciplinary diagnostic workflows and enabling more effective communication between

clinicians and patients.


This study has several limitations. First, the included conditions were not fully comprehensive or balanced,

and the model demonstrated limited accuracy and sensitivity for certain rare pathologies. Second, although

performance was evaluated using both automatic metrics and manual assessment, further validation in real-

world clinical settings is still required. Third, the model currently relies solely on IVCM images, which are
                                                                          S
insufficient to establish definitive diagnoses; future work should focus on developing multimodal
                                                                  ES
frameworks that integrate patient demographics, clinical history, and complementary examinations for more
                                                           PR
accurate diagnosis of corneal diseases. Fourth, age has been reported to influence sub-basal nerve

morphology and density, particularly in the infero-central whorl region, which may confound nerve-density
                                                  IN
assessments. Future studies should incorporate age as a covariate for model adjustment or statistical

                                        LE
correction to mitigate potential bias 48. Finally, as this was a single-center study, external validation across

                                    C
multiple centers will be necessary to confirm generalizability.
                              TI
In conclusion, we developed IVCM-Insight, an AI system that integrates image–text contrastive learning
                           R
                       A
with large language models to enable automated IVCM report generation and interactive clinical QA.

Validated through both automated metrics and expert assessment, the system demonstrated consistent

reliability in clinically relevant interpretation. These findings indicate that IVCM-Insight has the potential

to streamline clinical workflows, support diagnostic decision-making, enhance patient understanding, and

facilitate adoption in primary care, thereby addressing a critical gap in AI applications for IVCM.

Methods

As illustrated in Figure 1, the study workflow comprised four components: (1) collection of IVCM reports

and construction of the dataset; (2) development of IVCM-Insight, an AI model for automated IVCM report

generation and question–answering; (3) evaluation of report performance using natural language generation

(NLG) and multi-label classification (MLC) metrics; and (4) independent clinical assessment by three

ophthalmologists, who rated AI-generated reports and QA outputs.
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




Dataset Construction

This was a retrospective study conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Beijing Tongren Hospital,

Capital Medical University, between June 2022 and August 2025. The study protocol was approved by the

Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tongren Hospital (TREC2024-KY165) and adhered to the principles

of the Declaration of Helsinki, with informed consent waived due to its retrospective design.


All IVCM images were acquired using the Heidelberg Retina Tomograph III with Rostock Cornea Module

(Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). Examinations were performed using a standardized

scanning and reporting protocol. Prior to acquisition, the operator established a zero reference (z = 0 µm) at

the corneal epithelial interface after achieving stable contact and a clear superficial epithelial image using
                                                                         S
the coupling gel and contact cap. Then the cornea was scanned sequentially from the superficial to deep
                                                                 ES
layers in the following order: epithelium, Bowman’ s layer, stroma, Descemet’ s membrane, and endothelium.
                                                          PR
For each layer, the operator acquired at least 3–5 well-focused, high-contrast images with minimal motion

artifacts. Image sampling covered two predefined regions: (1) lesion-focused region, prioritizing the
                                                 IN
infiltration zone and/or the ulcer margin, and (2) control region, obtained from the peripheral clinically
                                        LE
normal cornea. During clinical reporting, representative images were selected to ensure coverage of each

                                    C
scanned layer and any lesion regions, resulting in 6–9 representative images per report, and the
                              TI
corresponding text report provided a description of findings at each corneal layer and across regions. Images
                          R
were captured at a resolution of 384 × 384 pixels and saved in JPEG format. Imaging depth was recorded as
                      A
the device-reported axial (z) position in micrometers (µm), with the zero reference (z = 0 µm) defined at the

corneal surface/epithelial interface. Images were subsequently obtained by advancing the focal plane along

the z-axis, with each frame corresponding to a depth value relative to the epithelial surface.


For each patient, images were organized by eye laterality (OD/OS) and imaging depth, and filenames were

assigned to encode metadata including patient ID, eye laterality, and image depth. For patients with bilateral

involvement, one eye was randomly selected for inclusion to avoid within-subject dependence. Clinical

reports were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records using patient IDs. Each

report was assigned a unique ID, and all corresponding images were organized under the folder named with

the same ID. Then a JSON file was constructed to maintain this correspondence, with each entry representing

a report-level sample and its associated image paths.
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




Patients who underwent IVCM examination for various corneal conditions during the study period were

included. Images from the corneal epithelium, SBNP, stroma, and endothelium were analyzed for features

including reduced sub-basal nerve density, stromal cell activation, and inflammatory cell infiltration, etc. To

align with clinically informative IVCM readouts, we ensured that SBNP-related features were explicitly

captured alongside epithelial and stromal findings. Descemet’s membrane, which is difficult to distinguish

during scanning, was included with the endothelial layer. Notably, “reduced sub-basal nerve density” was

treated as a qualitative descriptor extracted from clinician-written reports rather than a standardized

quantitative metric with units.


A rigorous three-step quality control (QC) protocol was applied: (1) Image QC: Images with poor focus, low
                                                                         S
contrast, motion artifacts, or other acquisition errors were excluded. (2) Report QC: Two ophthalmologists
                                                                  ES
reviewed clinical reports for spelling errors, incomplete descriptions, or inconsistencies; problematic reports
                                                          PR
were corrected or excluded. (3) Cross-checking: Consistency of images and clinical reports was confirmed

by a senior ophthalmologist.

Model Development
                                                 IN
                                        LE
The proposed AI system comprises two components: (1) a multimodal image–text framework using
                                    C
contrastive learning and matching objectives to generate structured clinical reports from IVCM images, and
                               TI
(2) an LLM-based module for interactive clinical question answering (QA). The multimodal image–text
                          R
framework aligns IVCM images with corresponding clinical reports to generate structured diagnostic outputs.
                      A
It employs dual-stream encoder architecture consisting of a visual encoder, text encoder, and cross-modal

decoder. Among them, a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone serves as the visual encoder, extracting patch-

level features from up to 9 IVCM images per patient. These features are aggregated by a multi-image fusion

module into a unified visual embedding. The text encoder, a transformer-based language model, encodes

clinical reports into contextualized embeddings. Finally, a cross-modal decoder integrates fused visual

embeddings with textual context to generate structured diagnostic reports.

The proposed framework was implemented in PyTorch within a distributed data-parallel setting and trained

using two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. The training pipeline started with the JSON file deserialized and a

sample list was created. For each sample, the image paths were read and transformed into a stacked tensor,

resulting in an input of shape (9, 3, 384, 384). A range of augmentation strategies was applied to each image
                                        ARTICLE IN PRESS




including color jitter (brightness = 0.2, contrast = 0.2, hue = 0.05, saturation = 0.1), random horizontal

flipping with a probability of 0.5, and random rotation within ±5°. The stacked multi-image tensor and the

corresponding report text were then jointly fed to the model.

The training process was formulated as a multi-task optimization problem with three objectives: (1) Image–

Text Contrastive Alignment Loss (ITC), a symmetric contrastive loss that aligns image and report

embeddings in a shared latent space; (2) Image-Conditioned Language Modeling (ICLM), where the decoder

generates report tokens conditioned on fused image embeddings and optimized via negative log-likelihood;

and (3) Joint Multi-Image Consistency Loss (MI), which enforces representational coherence across multiple

images. Model optimization followed a three-phase progressive schedule for 30 epochs: the vision encoder
                                                                           S
was frozen for the first 5 epochs, then joint training was performed for 15 epochs with advanced fusion
                                                                   ES
modules (spatial attention, temporal fusion, and adaptive pooling) initially frozen, and finally all modules

                                                            PR
were unfrozen for 10 epochs 49. Optimization uses AdamW 50 with weight decay 0.01 and gradient clipping

at 1.0. Base learning rate is 1e-4 with parameter-group multipliers of 0.1 (vision), 1.0 (text), and 2.0 (fusion),
                                                  IN
and the optimizer is re-created at phase transitions to reflect updated trainable parameters. The learning rate

                                         LE
follows a OneCycleLR schedule 51 over the total number of training steps, with max_lr set to the base

                                     C
learning rate and pct_start = 0.1. Early stopping 52 was employed on validation loss (patience 5, min_delta

0.001).
                               TI
                           R
An auxiliary regularization term was introduced to exploit inter-image correlations so that semantic
                       A
consistency among embeddings from the same patient visit could be enforced. The overall loss is formulated

as equation (1):


                                         (1) L=λ1 LITC +λ2 LICLM +λ3 LMI

where λ1 , λ2 , λ3 are task-specific weighting coefficients. Several optimization strategies were employed

to enhance convergence stability and training efficiency, including progressive learning, the OneCycle

learning rate scheduling policy, mixed-precision training 53 and early stopping.


An LLM-driven component was integrated into the system to facilitate clinical QA. A set of 30 representative

questions was designed based on common patient concerns and potential corneal conditions observed in the

dataset (Supplementary Table 4). Subsequently, a separate report-question-answer dataset was constructed
                                      ARTICLE IN PRESS




in JSON format using the same report IDs. Each entry included: (1) the report index and text, (2) a question

selected from our predefined question list, and (3) a clinician-validated answer. Following an evaluation of

several widely adopted LLMs such as the GPT-series, LLaMA-family, and ChatGLM, DeepSeek: 70B was

selected as the language backbone because it supports local deployment which is better for patient privacy

and data governance, and showed the best overall performance among the open-source candidates in our

internal benchmarking on the test dataset (Supplementary Table 1). A lightweight supervised fine-tuning

was performed with LoRA 54 to enhance its reasoning abilities and strengthen contextual understanding of

IVCM-specific findings.


Model Automatic Evaluation
                                                                        S
Automatic evaluation of the AI-generated reports was conducted using two complementary approaches:
                                                                ES
natural language generation (NLG) metrics and condition-based multi-label classification (MLC) metrics.
                                                         PR
For the NLG assessment, AI-generated reports were compared with reference reports using four established

                                                IN
indices. The Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU, 1–4) captured n-gram overlap to assess lexical

accuracy, whereas the Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation (ROUGE-L) evaluated structural
                                       LE
completeness by measuring the longest common subsequence. The Consensus-based Image Description
                                   C
Evaluation (CIDEr) emphasized agreement on clinically salient terms, while the Metric for Evaluation of
                             TI
Translation with Explicit ORdering (METEOR) provided precision and recall, incorporating synonyms and
                          R
word variants.        A
For the MLC evaluation, we aimed to assess whether the AI-generated reports accurately captured key

clinical findings compared with the reference clinical reports authored by ophthalmologists (Supplementary

Figure 1). We innovatively constructed a dictionary of 25 representative corneal conditions

(Supplementary Table 5), with each entry linked to a set of regular expressions. The dictionary was applied

to both AI-generated reports and original reports to extract condition labels, allowing each report to be

assigned multiple labels. Negation detection rules (e.g., “no”, “not seen”, or “absent”) were incorporated to

avoid false-positive matches. After automated label extraction, two ophthalmologists manually reviewed the

assigned labels to verify extraction accuracy and to correct mismatches. By aligning the extracted labels

from AI reports with those from original reports, we evaluated the model’s ability to preserve clinically

relevant information. Performance was quantified using accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and F1
                                       ARTICLE IN PRESS




score.


Model Manual Evaluation


To complement automated evaluation, we conducted a structured manual assessment of both AI‑generated

reports and QA outputs. For report assessment, 100 AI-generated reports with paired IVCM images and

reference clinical reports were randomly sampled from the test set and independently rated by three

ophthalmologists with more than five years of clinical experience and more than three years of IVCM image

interpretation experience. A study‑specific 5‑point Semantic Differential Scale (SDS; Supplementary Table

6) was used with four dimensions: accuracy, completeness, logical coherence, and diagnostic support.

Accuracy assessed whether clinical findings were faithfully represented; completeness examined whether
                                                                          S
essential information was sufficiently covered; logical coherence evaluated internal consistency of
                                                                  ES
descriptions; diagnostic support measured whether the report provided meaningful assistance for clinical

decision‑making.
                                                           PR
                                                  IN
To evaluate the QA component, we selected 20 reports that achieved top scores across all four dimensions,

ensuring representation of diverse corneal conditions. For each report, five representative questions were
                                        LE
crafted, yielding 100 QA pairs for evaluation. The same three ophthalmologists independently rated AI-
                                    C
                              TI
generated answers using a modified 5-point Semantic Differential Scale (SDS) across four dimensions:

                          R
accuracy, completeness, relevance, and non‑harmfulness. Accuracy reflected factual correctness;
                      A
completeness assessed whether answers sufficiently addressed the question; relevance evaluated contextual

appropriateness; and non-harmfulness captured clinical safety by ensuring that responses did not provide

misleading or unsafe information.


Report and QA assessments employed a 5-point scale (1 = poor, 5 = excellent), with analyses using the

average of three raters’ scores. For items scored ≤3, inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the intraclass

correlation coefficient (ICC, model 2,1), interpreted as <0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 =

good, and >0.90 = excellent 55.
