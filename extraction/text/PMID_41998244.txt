                OPEN             Input structure–driven instability
                                 and convergence in large language
                                 model clinical reasoning: a
                                 formative study using pediatric
                                 residency–level MCQs
                                 Vinson James, Catherine Caronia & Rajesh Savargaonkar
                                 Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly incorporated into medical education and clinical
                                 learning environments. While prior studies have focused on model accuracy on licensing-style
                                 examinations, less attention has been paid to the stability and reproducibility of LLM clinical reasoning
                                 under varying input structures—an issue central to safe educational and clinical deployment. To
                                 examine how question delivery structure influences performance stability, inter-model variability, and
                                 reproducibility of clinical reasoning across multiple contemporary LLMs using pediatric residency–level
                                 multiple-choice questions (MCQs). A standardized, evidence-based prompt was used to generate
                                 advanced-level pediatric USMLE Step 2/3–style MCQs emphasizing diagnostic reasoning, management
                                 decisions, and ethical judgment. One hundred draft MCQs generated using a standardized LLM
                                 prompt were randomly selected and independently reviewed by three pediatric physicians for medical
                                 accuracy, clinical realism, subspecialty relevance, and adherence to USMLE formatting. Twenty three
                                 questions were excluded by unanimous consensus, yielding a validated set of 77 MCQs. Six publicly
                                 available LLMs (ChatGPT, DeepSeek AI, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Perplexity AI, and OpenEvidence;
                                 October–December 2025 versions) were evaluated under two delivery conditions: (1) simultaneous
                                 presentation of all questions and (2) sequential delivery in batches of ten. Accuracy and inter-model
                                 variability were compared using paired t-tests and one-way ANOVA. When all questions were
                                 presented simultaneously, model accuracy varied widely (38%–90%), with significant inter-model
                                 differences, indicating poor reproducibility. In contrast, batch delivery resulted in marked convergence
                                 of performance across models (83%–88%), with no statistically significant inter-model differences.
                                 Sequential delivery in batches of ten substantially reduced performance dispersion and instability
                                 across all evaluated systems. LLM clinical reasoning performance is highly sensitive to input structure.
                                 Reducing contextual load through structured batch delivery improves reproducibility and minimizes
                                 inter-model variability, independent of model architecture. These findings suggest that prompt
                                 structure—rather than model selection alone—is a critical determinant of reliable LLM behavior and
                                 should be explicitly considered in the design of AI-supported medical education and assessment
                                 systems. This should be explicitly considered in the design of AI-supported medical education and
                                 assessment systems, particularly when LLMs are used as formative learning tools or clinical reasoning
                                 aids. Clinical Trial Number. The protocol was reviewed by the Office of the IRB at Good Samaritan

                                 Background and rationale
                                 Rapid advancements in LLMs have driven their adoption into medical education. Early studies report near-
                                 resident-level performance on surgical in-training exams1 and substantial subject-specific variability in
                                 biomedical sciences2.
                                     AI has also shown promise in clinical diagnostics, achieving pathologist-level accuracy in ulcerative colitis
                                 histology3 and matching clinician performance in wrist fracture detection4.
                                     Despite these successes, LLM accuracy is inconsistent and varies widely by task type, specialty, testing format,
                                 and prompt structure. Prior research has shown that LLMs are highly sensitive to prompt wording and structural
                                 changes5, vulnerable to hallucinations6, and susceptible to long-context failures due to attention dilution or
                                 token overload7–9.

                                 Conceptual framework
                                 This study is grounded in clarification-oriented educational research, as articulated by Bordage, which aims
                                 to explain why observed educational phenomena occur rather than merely describing outcomes or evaluating
                                 the effectiveness of a specific educational tool10. Within this framework, the present study integrates principles
                                 from Cognitive Load Theory and assessment theory to explain variability and instability in large language model
                                 (LLM) clinical reasoning performance.
                                     Cognitive Load Theory posits that learning and performance deteriorate when the cognitive demands
                                 of a task exceed the processing capacity of working memory, particularly when extraneous cognitive load is
                                 introduced11,12. Although LLMs operate at a scale far exceeding human cognition, they remain constrained by
                                 finite context windows and attention-based architectures. Prior research has demonstrated that extended or
                                 densely packed input sequences can lead to attention dilution, context degradation, and increased hallucination
                                 rates, all of which may be mitigated by reducing the volume of information processed simultaneously through
                                 batch delivery, especially during multi-step reasoning tasks that require sustained contextual coherence13,14.
                                     From an assessment theory perspective, high-quality multiple-choice examinations—such as the USMLE
                                 Step 2 and Step 3—are intentionally designed as discrete, itemized questions to minimize contextual interference
                                 between test items and reduce construct-irrelevant variance15,16. Presenting multiple clinical vignettes
                                 simultaneously violates this principle by introducing unnecessary extraneous load, which may impair both
                                 human and artificial reasoning processes by increasing interference across items.
                                     While this design choice may appear benign from a computational standpoint, it introduces construct-
                                 irrelevant variance that obscures whether observed performance differences reflect true reasoning ability or
                                 artifacts of input structure.
                                     Integrating these theoretical perspectives, we hypothesized that increasing contextual load through
                                 simultaneous question delivery would amplify inter-model variability and reduce reproducibility, whereas
                                 structured sequential delivery would stabilize performance by aligning input conditions with established
                                 principles of cognitive load management and assessment design. This conceptual framework informed both the
                                 study design and interpretation of results, positioning prompt structure rather than superficial formatting as a
                                 mechanistic determinant of LLM reliability.

                                 Objectives
                                 Primary objective was to examine how question delivery structure influences stability, reproducibility, and
                                 observed accuracy of LLM clinical reasoning across multiple platforms. Secondary objective is to look for
                                 consistency across clinical subdomains (cardiology, endocrinology, neurology, nephrology, pulmonology,
                                 infectious disease, emergency medicine) was evaluated as a secondary objective. A tertiary objective was to
                                 determine whether LLMs, like human examinees, are susceptible to construct-irrelevant variance introduced
                                 by test format and delivery structure. Demonstration of such susceptibility would have important implications
                                 for the validity of LLM-based assessments and would underscore the necessity of standardized, optimized input
                                 conditions when using these systems for educational evaluation or clinical decision support.

                                 Methods
                                 Study design
                                 A cross-sectional design was selected as appropriate for the formative objective of evaluating performance
                                 stability across conditions at a single point in time, without requiring longitudinal follow-up or intervention.
                                 This cross-sectional comparative study evaluated the performance, accuracy, and reproducibility of multiple
                                 large language models (LLMs) on a validated set of pediatric USMLE-style multiple-choice questions (MCQs).
                                 To assess both content quality and model consistency, we designed a two-phase approach: first, generating and
                                 validating a robust question set, and second, testing six contemporary LLMs under standardized conditions.

                                 MCQ generation, selection, and expert validation
                                 To ensure uniformity and reduce prompt-related variability, a known source of divergence in LLM-generated
                                 content, we used a standardized, evidence-based prompt to generate all MCQs5,17,18. Five LLMs were instructed
                                 to independently generate 100 advanced-level pediatric USMLE Step 2/3 style MCQs each, focusing on higher-
                                 order clinical reasoning, management decisions, and ethical judgment across pediatric subspecialties.
                                     From the resulting banks, the ChatGPT-generated set was selected for validation to ensure internal
                                 consistency of item style and difficulty, thereby isolating input structure effects during model evaluation. Using
                                 items from multiple generators would have introduced uncontrolled variability in question phrasing, complexity,
                                 and formatting, potentially confounding comparisons across delivery conditions.
                                     Prompt:

                                     “Create an advanced-level USMLE Step 2/3–style multiple-choice question in pediatrics using a realistic
                                 clinical vignette. Begin with the patient’s age, sex, and detailed history. Include relevant physical examination
                                 findings, vital signs, and—where appropriate—laboratory data (e.g., complete blood count, electrolytes,
                                 urinalysis), imaging (e.g., X-ray, ultrasound, CT, MRI), electrocardiograms, or biopsy results. Ensure the
                                 vignette assesses diagnostic reasoning, next best management, or ethical decision-making. The case may involve
                                 progression over time. Provide four to five answer choices, identify the correct answer, and include a concise
                                 explanation that clarifies the correct choice and outlines the rationale for excluding the incorrect options.
                                 Incorporate topics from pediatric subspecialties, including cardiology, endocrinology, neurology, nephrology,
                                 pulmonology, infectious disease, and emergency medicine. Avoid overly obvious clues and emphasize subtle
                                 clinical reasoning cues appropriate for NBME Step 2 CK or Step 3”.
                                     From the resulting question banks, the set generated by ChatGPT was selected for further validation to
                                 streamline the evaluation process. To minimize selection bias, we randomly selected 100 of these questions
                                 using a true random number generator (Random.org). A single source question set (ChatGPT-generated)
                                 was intentionally selected to ensure internal consistency of item style, difficulty, and linguistic characteristics.
                                 Using items from multiple generators would have introduced uncontrolled variability in question phrasing,
                                 complexity, and formatting factors known to influence LLM performance potentially confounding comparisons
                                 across delivery conditions19. This approach prioritizes internal validity, allowing isolation of input structure
                                 effects, though it may limit generalizability to questions generated by other LLMs or human experts.
                                     Three physicians with pediatric expertise independently evaluated each selected item: two U.S. board-
                                 certified pediatricians and one pediatric resident (U.S.-trained, with prior independent practice experience in
                                 India). Each question was assessed for medical accuracy, subspecialty relevance, clinical realism, and adherence
                                 to USMLE formatting conventions.
                                     Through unanimous consensus, 23 questions were excluded due to ambiguity, formatting issues, or flawed
                                 medical logic, resulting in a final validated set of 77 MCQs. Disagreements among reviewers were resolved
                                 through discussion until unanimous consensus was achieved; no additional adjudication was required as all
                                 excluded items met unanimous agreement. Only this physician-reviewed question set was used in subsequent
                                 LLM testing. In addition to accuracy and realism, reviewers qualitatively assessed each question’s difficulty level
                                 (basic vs. advanced) and cognitive complexity (recall vs. analysis/synthesis) to ensure alignment with Step 2/3
                                 expectations.

                                 Models evaluated and testing procedure
                                 We evaluated six publicly accessible LLMs: ChatGPT (GPT-4 Turbo, December 2025 version), Gemini (2025
                                 version), Microsoft Copilot (2025 version), Perplexity AI (2025 version), DeepSeek AI (2025 version), and
                                 OpenEvidence. All models were evaluated at default temperature settings (typically 0.7–1.0) as these reflect
                                 real-world user conditions.[33,34,35,36,37] System instructions were standardized across models, and no additional
                                 prompt engineering (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting) was employed to isolate input structure effects. Exact
                                 version identifiers and configuration details are provided in Table 3. [Table 3] Batch size of ten was selected
                                 pragmatically to approximate typical USMLE exam pacing (approximately one question per minute) while
                                 remaining within context window limits of evaluated models. This size also permits multiple batches without
                                 exceeding reasonable testing duration. Each model answered the same 77 validated MCQs under two distinct
                                 delivery formats: All-at-once format where all questions were presented in a single prompt and a batch format
                                 where questions were delivered sequentially in sets of ten.
                                     This dual-format design allowed us to examine whether input structure specifically, question volume and
                                 sequencing influenced model accuracy and response stability. Item-level variance and internal consistency
                                 were not formally assessed, as the primary outcome was accuracy aggregated across items; however, subdomain
                                 analyses provided exploratory insight into question-level variability. No models were subject to rate limits
                                 during testing, as all evaluations were completed in single sessions. All models successfully processed the full
                                 77-question set without token overflow errors, though we cannot exclude the possibility of internal truncation or
                                 context degradation in the all-at-once condition. Each model was evaluated once under each delivery condition,
                                 reflecting real-world use where users typically receive a single response per query.
                                     While prior studies have demonstrated LLM competency on medical examinations, these findings must
                                 be interpreted cautiously1,2. Most existing evaluations rely on single-model, single-prompt designs that fail to
                                 account for known vulnerabilities including prompt sensitivity, hallucination, and long-context degradation6,9,19.
                                 Moreover, the absence of standardized evaluation frameworks limits cross-study comparability and obscures
                                 whether reported performance reflects true reasoning ability versus artifacts of input structure. The present
                                 study addresses these gaps by systematically manipulating input structure while holding content constant across
                                 multiple models.
                                     However, we acknowledge that LLM outputs are inherently stochastic; future studies should employ multiple
                                 runs per condition to quantify within-model variability and establish confidence intervals around performance
                                 estimates.

                                 Blinding and scoring
                                 To minimize bias during both question validation and response scoring, all clinical evaluators and the study
                                 statistician were blinded to model identity. Physician reviewers scored model outputs against the predetermined
                                 correct answers established during expert validation without knowledge of which LLM produced each response.
                                 The supervising physician and data analyst remained similarly blinded throughout the scoring and initial
                                 analysis phases.

                                 Conceptual framework
                                 From an assessment theory perspective, high-quality multiple-choice examinations are intentionally designed
                                 as discrete, itemized questions to minimize contextual interference between test items and reduce construct-
                                 irrelevant variance19,20 Test-taking research in medical education has demonstrated that question order,
                                 sequencing effects, and item format can influence examinee performance independently of knowledge20. The
                                 observation that LLMs exhibit analogous sensitivity to input structure raises fundamental questions about
                                 whether these systems are performing context-independent reasoning or are merely exploiting statistical
                                 regularities in locally presented information—a distinction with profound implications for their deployment in
                                 high-stakes educational and clinical contexts.

                                 Outcome measures
                                 Our primary outcome was accuracy, defined as the percentage of correctly answered questions per model under
                                 each delivery condition. Consistency (reproducibility) was operationalized as the variability in performance
                                 across models within each testing format, quantified using the standard deviation of accuracy percentages.
                                 Lower variability indicated greater stability across models.

                                 Statistical analysis
                                 We used paired-samples *t*-tests to compare each model’s accuracy between the two delivery formats (all-at-
                                 once vs. batch). Assumptions of normality (Shapiro Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) were
                                 assessed and met prior to ANOVA. Inter-model differences in accuracy within each condition were assessed
                                 using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For post hoc pairwise comparisons, we applied a significance
                                 threshold of *p* < 0.01, with statistically significant comparisons indicated by an asterisk (*). All primary
                                 analyses were two-sided, with *p* < 0.01 considered statistically significant.

                                 IRB exemption; no patient data; reviewers, participants statisticians were blinded. Conforms to responsible AI
                                 use frameworks.

                                 performance (cardiology, endocrinology, etc.) showed similar patterns of convergence under batch conditions,
                                 though sample sizes per subdomain were insufficient for formal statistical comparison.

                                 Consistency in the All-at-Once Condition
                                 Model performance showed substantial heterogeneity. Accuracy ranged from 38% (Gemini) to 90% (Perplexity).
                                 DeepSeek demonstrated high performance (87%), while ChatGPT (GPT-4 Turbo) achieved moderate accuracy
                                 (62%). Copilot (GPT-4 Bing implementation) scored 44%. ANOVA demonstrated significant differences across
                                 models (F(4,380) = 23.441, ρ < 0.001), confirming poor inter-model consistency5,18,21.

                                 Consistency in the 10-at-a-Time Condition
                                 When questions were administered in smaller batches, accuracy markedly improved and converged across
                                 models, ranging from 83% to 88%. Copilot achieved the highest accuracy (88%), followed closely by ChatGPT,
                                 DeepSeek, and Perplexity (86–87%). Gemini and OpenEvidence performed at 83%. ANOVA demonstrated no
                                 significant differences (F(5,456) = 0.294, ρ = 0.916), indicating high consistency among models under structured
                                 input conditions.

                                 Accuracy analysis
                                 Pairwise accuracy comparisons demonstrated substantial instability in the all-at-once condition, whereas
                                 reproducibility improved markedly under structured batch delivery22,23.

                                 Discussion
                                 Our findings suggest that delivering MCQs in smaller batches may reduce hallucination risk by mitigating
                                 context overload, though hallucination rates were not directly measured in this study and stabilizes model
                                 performance, likely due to reducing the volume of information processed simultaneously, which metaphorically
                                 parallels cognitive load reduction in humans and improved attention span within transformer architectures. By
                                 limiting the amount of information processed at once, models can maintain contextual coherence and reduce
                                 errors associated with long-context degradation. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, only a
                                 single set of 100 MCQs was used, which may not capture the full spectrum of residency-level content. Second,
                                 the questions were pediatric-focused, and findings may not generalize to other specialties or adult medicine.
                                 Third, models were evaluated at a single point in time, and future version updates or model drift may alter
                                 performance. Finally, although LLMs demonstrate substantial accuracy, their outputs should not replace human
                                 oversight in medical education; ethical implementation requires supervision and critical appraisal by qualified
                                 educators to ensure safe and effective learning outcomes.

                                 Cognitive load and context window effects
                                 The observed decline in performance under the all-at-once condition reflects the well-established limitations
                                 of large language models when processing extended sequences. Specifically, long input sequences can lead to
                                 attention collapse7, context overflow and degradation8,9, and amplification of hallucinations under increased
                                 cognitive load6,24. The observed decline in performance under the all-at-once condition reflects well-documented
                                 limitations of transformer architectures when processing extended sequences. We invoke cognitive load theory
                                 here as a useful pedagogical metaphor rather than a claim of mechanistic equivalence: just as humans experience
                                 performance degradation when extraneous cognitive demands exceed working memory capacity, LLMs
                                 exhibit attention collapse, context degradation, and increased hallucination rates when input sequences exceed
                                 optimal processing thresholds9,25,26. The underlying mechanisms differ fundamentally attention mechanisms
                                 in transformers versus working memory limitations in humans but the functional consequence (degraded
                                 performance under high-input load) is analogous and useful for motivating input structure optimization27.
                                 These findings highlight the sensitivity of LLMs to input length and structural complexity, emphasizing the
                                 importance of optimized prompt design for accurate medical reasoning.

                                 Identified model weaknesses
                                 Several consistent weaknesses were identified across the evaluated models. Models frequently produced variable
                                 outputs across identical input sets, reflecting version-to-version unpredictability documented in drift studies22.
                                 The tendency to generate incorrect or fabricated information was evident, consistent with prior reports in
                                 both medical chatbots and general LLMs6,24. Minor differences in question phrasing significantly influenced
                                 model outputs, a phenomenon previously observed in clinical applications such as Med-PaLM5,17. Variations in
                                 training data, tokenization strategies, and internal reasoning pathways contributed to cross-model differences in
                                 accuracy and reliability28. We distinguish between model-based limitations (architectural constraints, training

                                 Contributions of this study
                                 This study provides several key contributions to the field of AI-assisted medical education:

                                  1. Evidence-based strategies to improve reliability: Administering questions in structured batches of ten re-
                                     duced hallucinations and stabilized accuracy, consistent with retrieval-augmented generation and struc-
                                     tured-prompting frameworks18,21.
                                  2. Reproducible evaluation framework: The study establishes a standardized, multi-specialty benchmarking
                                     approach suitable for LLM performance assessment.
                                  3. Alignment with real-world testing: The batch-based format mirrors itemized USMLE Step 2/3 examinations,
                                     enhancing the ecological validity of results.
                                  4. Foundation for safe educational integration: By identifying optimal input strategies, this framework supports
                                     responsible deployment of AI systems in medical training environments29.

                                 Our findings suggest that delivering MCQs in smaller batches mitigates hallucinations and stabilizes model
                                 performance, likely due to reduced cognitive load and improved attention span within transformer architectures.
                                 By limiting the amount of information processed at once, models can maintain contextual coherence and
                                 reduce errors associated with long-context degradation. Beyond evaluation, the validated MCQ generation
                                 methodology described here represents a powerful pedagogical tool. When guided by structured prompting
                                 approaches similar to those demonstrated in studies, LLMs can generate limitless practice questions tailored to
                                 specific curricula, learning objectives, and trainee levels9,25,30. However, this capability carries inherent risks: AI-
                                 generated content may propagate misinformation or contribute to infodemics, particularly in rapidly evolving
                                 clinical contexts such as the COVID-19 pandemic, where guidelines changed frequently and conflicting evidence
                                 emerged30–32. The physician review process employed in this study, rigorous expert validation by independent
                                 reviewers, therefore represents not merely a research step but an essential governance model for any educational
                                 deployment. This human in the loop safeguard ensures that AI-generated content is vetted for accuracy, clinical
                                 relevance, and alignment with current standards before reaching learners. Our findings thus provide both the
                                 stable engine (batch-optimized processing) and the quality control blueprint (expert validation) necessary
                                 for building trustworthy, scalable medical education tools. Importantly, while batch delivery substantially
                                 improves reproducibility, it does not eliminate the risk of factual errors. Even at 88% accuracy, the remaining
                                 incorrect responses could have clinical significance if deployed without oversight. This limitation underscores
                                 the necessity of integrating scalable fact-checking mechanisms into LLM-based medical reasoning systems.
                                 Recent advances in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) frameworks, such as TrumorGPT, demonstrate
                                 promising approaches to grounding LLM responses in verified medical sources30. By implementing a RAG layer
                                 that retrieves and synthesizes information from vetted, up-to-date medical literature—such as peer-reviewed
                                 guidelines, UpToDate, or specialty-specific textbooks—before finalizing an answer, educational AI systems could
                                 incorporate a built-in verification step that substantially reduces factual errors. This architectural consideration
                                 should be viewed as complementary to the input structure optimization demonstrated in the present study;
                                 together, stable input formatting and retrieval-augmented generation form a dual foundation for reliable AI-
                                 mediated medical education.
                                     Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, only a single set of 100 MCQs was used, which may
                                 not capture the full spectrum of residency-level content. Second, the questions were pediatric-focused, and
                                 findings may not generalize to other specialties or adult medicine. Third, models were evaluated at a single
                                 point in time, and future version updates or model drift may alter performance. Fourth, this study did not
                                 include a human control group of pediatric residents completing the same questions under identical delivery
                                 conditions. Such a comparison would have provided valuable context for interpreting the clinical significance
                                 of the observed performance differences and variability patterns. It remains unknown whether residents would
                                 demonstrate similar sensitivity to question delivery format, though cognitive load theory would predict some
                                 degree of performance degradation under simultaneous presentation and whether the convergence observed
                                 under batch conditions approaches or exceeds human-level consistency. Future studies should include parallel
                                 human testing to establish clinically meaningful benchmarks for both accuracy and reproducibility. Without
                                 such comparators, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating relative stability differences across input
                                 structures rather than absolute measures of clinical reasoning capacity. Fifth, we examined only a single batch
                                 size (ten items) and a fixed presentation order. It remains unknown whether smaller batches (e.g., five items)
                                 would further improve performance, whether larger batches (e.g., 15–20 items) would maintain the observed
                                 stability, or whether item sequencing effects such as question difficulty ordering or topic intermixing influence
                                 LLM performance in ways analogous to human test-taking. Systematic variation of batch size and randomized
                                 Monte Carlo simulations across multiple presentation orders would provide more robust estimates of input
                                 structure effects and should be prioritized in future research. Sixth, using only ChatGPT-generated questions,
                                 while ensuring internal consistency, may limit generalizability to items generated by other LLMs or written by
                                 human experts; future studies should compare performance across question sources.
                                     Finally, although LLMs demonstrate substantial accuracy, their outputs should not replace human oversight
                                 in medical education; ethical implementation requires supervision and critical appraisal by qualified educators
                                 to ensure safe and effective learning outcomes.

                                  2. Evaluation in image-intensive or OSCE-style, open-ended clinical scenarios.

                                 Conclusion
                                 Performance of large language models on residency-level MCQs is highly sensitive and dependent on the
                                 input structure. Delivering all questions simultaneously leads to substantial variability and instability, whereas
                                 structured delivery in batches of ten promotes reproducible and consistent results across models. These findings
                                 provide an actionable, evidence-based framework for the safe and effective integration of AI tools into medical
                                 education, underscoring the importance of standardized prompt design and controlled testing conditions. LLMs
                                 can augment medical education but must be deployed under human supervision within structured frameworks
                                 to ensure safe, effective, and ethically responsible use.
                                     For educators and exam designers, these findings provide an actionable guideline: delivering questions
                                 in structured batches of ten substantially improves LLM response reproducibility and reduces cross-model
                                 variability. When combined with expert validation and retrieval-augmented factchecking, this approach
                                 supports the safe integration of AI into medical education as a formative learning tool rather than an autonomous
                                 assessment system.
