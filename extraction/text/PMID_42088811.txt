                                                Abstract
                                                Agentic artificial intelligence (AI) systems are emerging as a transformative approach in healthcare, enabling
                                                autonomous task execution through integrated reasoning and tool use. While early implementations have
                                                largely relied on large language models (LLMs), growing evidence suggests that smaller language models may
                                                be better suited for many healthcare workflows due to their efficiency, scalability, and practicality in real-
                                                world clinical environments. This review examines the current landscape of small language models (SLMs)
                                                used in agentic healthcare applications, including clinical documentation, decision support, patient triage,
                                                and administrative automation. We synthesize available evidence on their performance, safety, and
                                                economic implications, and discuss key considerations for clinical deployment, including regulatory
                                                alignment and governance. Overall, small language models appear to offer sufficient capability for most
                                                agentic healthcare tasks while providing meaningful advantages in deployability, cost, and operational
                                                efficiency, supporting their role as a viable and often preferable alternative for clinical implementation.

                                                Introduction And Background
                                                The healthcare industry stands at the threshold of a transformative technological revolution driven by
                                                agentic artificial intelligence (AI) systems. Unlike traditional AI applications that operate as passive tools
                                                requiring constant human oversight, agentic AI systems function as autonomous controllers capable of
                                                perceiving environmental conditions, formulating strategic plans, and executing complex goal-directed
                                                tasks through sophisticated tool integration [1]. This paradigm shift represents a fundamental reimagining
                                                of how artificial intelligence can augment and enhance healthcare delivery, moving beyond simple pattern
                                                recognition to comprehensive workflow automation and decision support. In this review, agentic artificial
                                                intelligence is defined as systems capable of autonomous tool calling, multi-step planning, and structured
                                                output generation without continuous human prompting.


                                                The emergence of large language models (LLMs) initially dominated the agentic AI landscape, with systems
                                                such as GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini demonstrating remarkable capabilities in natural language
                                                understanding, reasoning, and tool manipulation [2]. However, the healthcare sector's unique requirements,
                                                including stringent latency constraints, cost sensitivity, privacy imperatives, and regulatory compliance
                                                demands, have exposed significant limitations in LLM-based approaches. The computational intensity of
                                                large models, with parameter counts often exceeding 70 billion, creates substantial barriers to real-time
                                                deployment in clinical environments where millisecond response times can be critical for patient safety [3].


                                                Small language models (SLMs), typically defined as systems with fewer than 10 billion parameters, have

                                                 emerged as a compelling alternative that addresses many of these limitations while maintaining sufficient
                                                 capability for healthcare applications [4]. Recent technical advances, including the NVIDIA Nemotron Nano
                                                 2 family with hybrid Mamba-Transformer architectures, demonstrate that SLMs can achieve comparable
                                                 performance to much larger models while offering dramatic improvements in throughput, energy efficiency,
                                                 and deployment flexibility [5]. The Salesforce xLAM-2 series further validates this trend, showing state-of-
                                                 the-art performance on function calling benchmarks with models as small as 8 billion parameters [6].


                                                 The healthcare domain presents particularly favorable conditions for SLM deployment due to the prevalence
                                                 of structured, repetitive tasks that characterize most clinical workflows. Electronic health record (EHR)
                                                 interactions, clinical documentation, patient triage protocols, medication management, and administrative
                                                 processes often involve well-defined procedures with predictable input-output relationships [7]. These
                                                 characteristics align well with SLM capabilities, which excel at bounded transformations with clear schemas
                                                 and deterministic outputs while struggling less with the open-ended reasoning challenges that favor larger
                                                 models.


                                                 Economic considerations further strengthen the case for SLM adoption in healthcare settings. Healthcare
                                                 organizations face increasing pressure to control costs while improving the quality and accessibility of care
                                                 [8]. The 10-30× cost reduction achievable through SLM deployment, combined with the ability to run models
                                                 on-premises or at the edge, offers significant advantages for resource-constrained environments, including
                                                 rural hospitals, community health centers, and international development contexts [9]. This economic
                                                 efficiency enables broader democratization of AI-powered healthcare tools, potentially addressing health
                                                 equity concerns that arise when advanced technologies remain accessible only to well-funded institutions.


                                                 Regulatory frameworks are evolving to accommodate AI integration in healthcare, with recent guidance
                                                 from the FDA's Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCP) for AI-enabled devices, the European Union's AI
                                                 Act implementation, and the World Health Organization's regulatory considerations for AI in health [10-12].
                                                 These frameworks emphasize the importance of transparency, accountability, and continuous monitoring
                                                 requirements that may be more readily achievable with smaller, more interpretable models that can be
                                                 thoroughly validated and monitored in clinical environments.


                                                 Despite these promising developments, the evidence base for SLM effectiveness in healthcare applications
                                                 remains fragmented and heterogeneous. Previous reviews have focused primarily on large language models
                                                 or have provided only superficial coverage of small model capabilities [13]. No comprehensive systematic
                                                 review has specifically examined the evidence for SLM-based agentic systems in healthcare contexts,
                                                 creating a critical knowledge gap that limits evidence-based decision-making by healthcare leaders,
                                                 technology implementers, and regulatory bodies.


                                                 This systematic review addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive analysis of current evidence
                                                 regarding SLM effectiveness. We examine technical performance metrics, clinical validation studies,
                                                 economic evaluations, and regulatory compliance considerations to provide actionable guidance for
                                                 healthcare organizations considering SLM implementation. Our analysis includes both peer-reviewed
                                                 research and high-quality technical reports to capture the rapidly evolving landscape of SLM development
                                                 and deployment.


                                                 The implications of this research extend beyond immediate technical considerations to fundamental
                                                 questions about the future of healthcare delivery. As healthcare systems worldwide grapple with workforce
                                                 shortages, rising costs, and increasing demand for services, agentic AI systems powered by efficient SLMs
                                                 may provide a pathway to sustainable, scalable, and equitable healthcare enhancement. Understanding the
                                                 evidence base for these technologies is essential for responsible innovation that prioritizes patient safety
                                                 while maximizing societal benefit.


                                                 Review
                                                 Study design and registration
                                                 This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic
                                                 Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [14]. A review protocol was developed a priori to
                                                 guide the study design, eligibility criteria, and analysis, following established methodological standards for
                                                 systematic reviews of artificial intelligence interventions in healthcare [15].


                                                 Search strategy and information sources
                                                 We developed a comprehensive search strategy in consultation with a medical librarian and an AI research
                                                 specialist. The search encompassed four major databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, arXiv preprint server, IEEE
                                                 Xplore Digital Library, and Google Scholar. The search period extended from January 1, 2020, through
                                                 August 31, 2025, to capture both foundational work in language model development and the most recent
                                                 advances in small language model applications.

                                                    Eligibility criteria
                                                    Studies were included if they examined small language models (≤10 billion parameters) in healthcare
                                                    applications and investigated agentic AI capabilities relevant to healthcare. Studies were required to
                                                    demonstrate at least one core agentic capability, defined as autonomous tool integration, multi-step
                                                    reasoning, or independent workflow execution. Eligible study designs included clinical validation studies,
                                                    technical evaluations, economic analyses, and systematic reviews. We included peer-reviewed articles, high-
                                                    quality preprints with detailed methodology, and official technical reports from established organizations
                                                    published in English between January 2020 and August 2025. Research was required to provide quantitative
                                                    outcomes, qualitative assessments, or comprehensive analytical frameworks (Table 2).

                                                                          Study selection process
                                                                          Two independent reviewers (ZK and research assistant) conducted the study selection process using a
                                                                          standardized approach. Initial screening involved title and abstract review using predetermined inclusion
                                                                          and exclusion criteria. Full-text review was performed for all potentially eligible studies, with disagreements
                                                                          resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer when necessary. The selection process
                                                                          was managed using Covidence systematic review software to ensure transparency and reproducibility. The
                                                                          study selection process is summarized in Figure 1 [11]. Database searches identified 1,106 records in total:
                                                                          PubMed/MEDLINE, n = 312; arXiv, n = 487; IEEE Xplore, n = 94; and Google Scholar, n = 213. Searches
                                                                          through other methods yielded a further 58 records (citation searching, n = 28; reference list review, n = 19;
                                                                          and expert recommendations, n = 11), for a combined total of 1,164 records. Following removal of 218
                                                                          duplicate records, 888 records proceeded to title and abstract screening (1,164 - 218 - 58 out-of-scope
                                                                          imports = 888). After title/abstract screening, 695 records were excluded (not relevant to SLMs or healthcare,
                                                                          n = 512, and no agentic AI component, n = 183), leaving 193 reports sought for full-text retrieval. Of these,
                                                                          12 could not be retrieved due to inaccessibility, leaving 181 reports assessed for full-text eligibility. Full-text
                                                                          review excluded a further 146 reports: LLMs > 10 billion only with no SLM comparison, n = 67; no healthcare
                                                                          application, n = 28; opinion or editorial without empirical data, n = 22; insufficient methodological detail, n
                                                                          = 18; duplicate publication, n = 7; and non-English language, n = 4. This yielded 35 studies included in the
                                                                          final review (181 - 146 = 35). Inter-rater reliability exceeded the pre-specified threshold of κ > 0.70 at both
                                                                          screening stages before proceeding to data extraction [14]. The characteristics of the 35 included studies are

                                                  Data extraction and management
                                                  Data extraction was performed using a standardized form developed specifically for this review and pilot-
                                                  tested on five studies. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, publication year, study design,
                                                  setting, sample size, and follow-up duration), model specifications (architecture, parameter count, training
                                                  data, fine-tuning approaches, and deployment configuration), healthcare applications (clinical domain,
                                                  specific use cases, integration with existing systems, and user populations), technical performance (accuracy
                                                  metrics, latency measurements, throughput data, energy consumption, and cost analyses), clinical
                                                  outcomes (patient safety measures, workflow efficiency, user satisfaction, and clinical effectiveness),
                                                  economic evaluations (cost-effectiveness ratios, budget impact analyses, and return on investment
                                                  calculations), and regulatory considerations (compliance frameworks, approval status, and safety
                                                  monitoring approaches).


                                                  Methodological rationale and decision framework
                                                  Conceptual Framing


                                                  The research question was framed specifically around SLMs (≤10 billion parameters) in agentic contexts,
                                                  rather than general medical AI, because agentic behavior (autonomous tool calling, multi-step planning,
                                                  and structured output generation) is the capability set most directly translatable to measurable clinical
                                                  workflow impact [1,6,10]. The search period opened in January 2020 because reliable agentic SLM
                                                  benchmarks did not exist prior to the transformer scaling era, and meaningful healthcare deployment
                                                  studies did not appear before this date [2,3].


                                                  Database and Source Selection


                                                  PubMed/MEDLINE was selected as the primary biomedical source. arXiv was included because the AI field
                                                  publishes critical technical advances as preprints 12-18 months ahead of journal publication; excluding
                                                  arXiv would systematically undercount SLM developments, particularly from industry groups [4,5]. IEEE
                                                  Xplore captures biomedical engineering conference proceedings not consistently indexed in PubMed.
                                                  Google Scholar provided a supplementary sweep for grey literature and technical reports. Citation searching
                                                  and expert consultation were included as Column 2 sources per the PRISMA 2020 framework [11] to reduce
                                                  publication bias.


                                                  PICO Operationalization


                                                  The ≤10 billion parameter threshold was applied strictly; where model size was reported as a range, the
                                                  upper bound was used. Studies using quantized or pruned models were included if the deployed parameter
                                                  count fell at or below 10 billion. A broad outcome set was pre-specified deliberately: restricting outcomes to
                                                  patient-level clinical endpoints would have excluded nearly all eligible studies, given the early-stage nature
                                                  of agentic SLM deployment in healthcare [6,8]. Heterogeneous comparators were accepted, including rule-
                                                  based systems, larger LLMs, and standard clinical workflows, to reflect real-world implementation diversity
                                                  [7,9].


                                                  Study Selection and Inter-rater Reliability


                                                  Three reviewers were involved because SLM terminology is inconsistent in the literature and borderline
                                                  cases were anticipated. Cohen's kappa (κ) was computed after each screening stage; a threshold of κ > 0.70
                                                  was required before proceeding, consistent with established systematic review standards [14]. Any κ below
                                                  this threshold would have triggered a calibration session and re-screening of a random 20% sample.
                                                  Covidence was used for study management, with per-database record counts logged manually before import.


                                                  Data Extraction Pilot


                                                  The extraction form was pilot-tested on five studies spanning the range of anticipated designs. Pilot testing
                                                  identified ambiguity in the "healthcare setting" field, which was revised to require a specific institution type
                                                  before full extraction commenced. Where model size was not explicitly stated, it was inferred from
                                                  architecture descriptions and cross-referenced against publicly available model cards.

                                                  be matched to study design [12,13,15]. No single tool adequately covers the full range of study designs
                                                  encountered in this review, which is why the additional custom framework was developed for technical

                                                                       Synthesis Strategy


                                                                       A mixed-methods approach was chosen because the literature is too heterogeneous for pooled meta-analysis
                                                                       across all outcomes. Quantitative meta-analysis using random-effects models was performed where studies
                                                                       reported directly comparable metrics on the same clinical tasks. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified
                                                                       using I²; values >50% triggered subgroup analysis by model size (1-3 billion, 3-7 billion, and 7-10 billion
                                                                       parameters) and application type. Narrative synthesis was used for outcomes too diverse for quantitative
                                                                       pooling [14]. Economic data were converted to 2024 USD using purchasing power parity adjustment factors.


                                                                       Quality assessment
                                                                       Quality assessment was tailored to the study design using established instruments. No copyright fees or
                                                                       formal written permissions are required for any of the tools below for academic research use.


                                                                       The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool Version 2 (RoB 2) was pre-specified for randomized controlled trials. No RCTs
                                                                       were identified among the included studies, so this tool was not applied. This tool is freely available under
                                                                       Creative Commons licensing via the Cochrane Collaboration (riskofbias.info); no permission is required for
                                                                       research use.


                                                                       Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) was used for non-randomized studies
                                                                       [12]. It is published with open access in the BMJ and freely available from the Cochrane Bias Methods Group;
                                                                       no written permission is required for research use.


                                                                       A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews (AMSTAR-2) was applied to systematic reviews identified
                                                                       within the included literature [13]. It is freely available at amstar.ca; no permission or licensing fee is
                                                                       required for academic use.


                                                                       Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards for Artificial Intelligence (CHEERS-AI) was
                                                                       used for economic evaluations [15]. It is published open access under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 and is free for
                                                                       academic use with citation. Commercial use requires explicit permission from Elsevier/ISPOR.


                                                                       A custom quality assessment framework was developed de novo by the research team for technical reports
                                                                       and preprints not covered by the above validated tools. Four dimensions were evaluated: (a) methodology
                                                                       transparency and reproducibility, (b) validation rigor, (c) independence from commercial conflicts of
                                                                       interest, and (d) completeness of reporting. No copyright restrictions apply. Two reviewers independently
                                                                       assessed study quality, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Studies were categorized as high,
                                                                       moderate, or low quality based on overall risk of bias.

                                                           Data synthesis and analysis
                                                           Given the heterogeneity of study designs, populations, and outcomes, we employed a mixed-methods
                                                           synthesis approach combining quantitative meta-analysis where appropriate with narrative synthesis for
                                                           diverse outcomes. Meta-analysis was performed for studies with comparable outcomes using random-effects
                                                           models to account for expected heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics, with
                                                           values >50% indicating substantial heterogeneity requiring investigation through subgroup analysis or
                                                           meta-regression. Thematic analysis was conducted for studies with diverse outcomes or insufficient data for
                                                           meta-analysis. Synthesis followed established frameworks for complex interventions, organizing findings by
                                                           technical capabilities, clinical applications, economic considerations, and implementation factors. Planned
                                                           subgroup analyses examined differences by healthcare setting (hospital versus ambulatory), model size
                                                           categories (1-3 billion, 3-7 billion, and 7-10 billion parameters), and application type (documentation,
                                                           decision support, and administrative tasks).


                                                           Results
                                                           The systematic search and screening process yielded 35 studies that met all eligibility criteria for evaluating
                                                           agentic small language models in healthcare settings. The included literature encompasses a diverse range
                                                           of study designs, clinical applications, and technical evaluations, reflecting the rapid and heterogeneous
                                                           development of these systems. To provide a structured synthesis of this emerging field, the findings are
                                                           organized into three primary domains. First, we examine the technical performance and core agentic
                                                           capabilities of small language models across various tasks. Second, we evaluate their practical integration
                                                           into clinical workflows and specific healthcare applications. Finally, we analyze the economic and
                                                           operational considerations associated with their deployment, highlighting the resource implications for
                                                           healthcare organizations.


                                                           Technical Performance and Agentic Capabilities


                                                           Across the included studies, agentic small language models demonstrated variable technical performance
                                                           depending on task complexity and deployment context. Several studies reported that smaller agentic
                                                           models achieved performance comparable to larger language models for narrowly scoped tasks, particularly
                                                           in documentation and administrative workflows. Frequently reported advantages included reduced latency,
                                                           lower computational requirements, and improved feasibility for deployment within healthcare
                                                           infrastructure.


                                                           Performance consistency decreased for tasks requiring broader contextual reasoning or multi-domain
                                                           inference. Evaluation approaches varied substantially across studies, limiting direct quantitative comparison
                                                           but highlighting trade-offs between efficiency, scalability, and generalizability.


                                                           Clinical Applications and Workflow Integration


                                                           Clinical applications spanned documentation assistance, workflow automation, decision support, and
                                                           patient triage. Studies evaluating documentation and administrative tasks consistently reported
                                                           improvements in workflow efficiency and reductions in clinician time burden. In contrast, studies focused
                                                           on clinical decision support demonstrated more variable outcomes, with performance and acceptance
                                                           influenced by the degree of human oversight and system transparency.

                                                 Studies incorporating clinician-in-the-loop designs generally reported higher usability and trust, suggesting
                                                 that hybrid deployment strategies may be more appropriate for near-term clinical adoption.


                                                 Economic and Operational Considerations


                                                 Economic analyses suggested that agentic small language models may offer operational advantages over
                                                 larger models and traditional systems, particularly for high-volume, repetitive tasks. Reported benefits
                                                 included lower infrastructure requirements and reduced operational costs. However, economic outcomes
                                                 were highly context-dependent, with integration costs and regulatory requirements influencing overall cost-
                                                 effectiveness. Long-term economic evaluations were limited across studies.


                                                 Study quality and evidence gaps
                                                 Overall, the methodological quality of included studies was moderate. Common limitations included small
                                                 sample sizes, limited external validation, and heterogeneity in outcome reporting. These findings
                                                 underscore the need for standardized evaluation frameworks and longitudinal studies to better assess safety,
                                                 effectiveness, and economic impact.


                                                 Summary of findings
                                                 Taken together, the included studies suggest that agentic small language models have the potential to
                                                 enhance efficiency, scalability, and cost-effectiveness in healthcare workflows, particularly for well-defined
                                                 operational tasks. However, variability in study design and evaluation approaches limits definitive
                                                 conclusions across clinical domains. Careful task selection, constrained autonomy, and clinician oversight
                                                 emerged as consistent factors associated with successful implementation.


                                                 Conclusions
                                                 Small language models represent a meaningful advancement in healthcare AI, offering a balance of
                                                 technical capability, efficiency, and deployability that addresses many limitations of larger models. Current
                                                 evidence suggests that SLMs can support core agentic functions, including structured output generation,
                                                 tool integration, and workflow automation, with performance that is sufficient for many clinical and
                                                 administrative use cases. Their advantages in latency, cost, and infrastructure requirements make them
                                                 particularly well-suited for real-world healthcare environments, including resource-constrained settings.
                                                 These characteristics position SLMs as a practical and scalable option for expanding access to AI-driven
                                                 healthcare solutions.


                                                 However, successful implementation depends on careful alignment with clinical needs, appropriate task
                                                 selection, and the integration of human oversight for complex decision-making. The current evidence base
                                                 remains limited by heterogeneity in study design, small sample sizes, and a lack of long-term clinical
                                                 outcome data. Future research should prioritize standardized evaluation frameworks, randomized controlled
                                                 trials, and real-world implementation studies to better assess safety, effectiveness, and economic impact.
                                                 Continued regulatory development and governance will also be essential to ensure safe and equitable
                                                 deployment. Overall, SLMs offer strong potential but require rigorous validation to support widespread
                                                 clinical adoption.
