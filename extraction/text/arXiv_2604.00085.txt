Figure 1: Overview of the CAMP framework. Given a clinical note and candidate diagnoses, the attending
physician first renders an initial diagnostic judgment, then assembles a case-adaptive specialist panel with directed
focus areas. Each specialist quotes evidence from the note and casts a three-valued vote. A hybrid router directs
each diagnosis to one of three resolution paths: strong consensus decisions are applied directly, weak consensus
falls back to the attending physician’s initial judgment, and conflicts are escalated for evidence-based arbitration.

    Case 10144089-DS-20:
    Subarachnoid Hemorrhage with Anatomical Ambiguity

 Case Summary. The patient (F) presented with unresponsiveness and agonal breathing after a fall. Transfer notes show
 SBPs up to 240s. CT demonstrated a large left-sided aneurysm with diffuse hemorrhage; subsequent CTA confirmed an
 11 mm midline shift and signs of herniation (newly blown right pupil). Clinical findings strongly support diagnoses of SAH
 and cerebral edema with mass effect.
    Gold Diagnoses: Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Left MCA aneurysm, Cerebral edema with compression.


     Subarachnoid hemorrhage
     C ONSENSUS : K EEP
     Neurosurgeon → KEEP (1.0) — “CTA again demonstrates. . . interval increase in hemorrhage in lateral, [third] and
     [fourth] ventricles.” Extensive intraventricular blood is a hallmark of SAH from a large ruptured aneurysm.
     Neurointensivist → KEEP (1.0) — “CT scan demonstrated a large (2.5 × 3.5) [aneurysm] with [hemorrhage].” Acute
     onset with GCS 3T and imaging findings confirm a catastrophic SAH.
     Vascular Neurologist → KEEP (1.0) — “Diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage along bilateral cerebral hemispheres.” The
     diffuse pattern of hemorrhage is consistent with a high-pressure aneurysmal rupture.


     Left MCA aneurysm
     C ONFLICT → A RBITRATION : ACCEPT
     Neurosurgeon → KEEP (1.0) — “CTA again demonstrates a large L-sided anterior aneurysm.” Clinically, a large
     aneurysm in the anterior circulation is categorized as MCA or ICA terminus; supports this classification.
     Neurointensivist → REMOVE (1.0) — “Large (2.5 × 3.5) left sided aneurysm.” Note only specifies ‘L-sided anterior’;
     lacks explicit ‘MCA’ anatomical confirmation, thus removing specific label per strict criteria.
     Vascular Neurologist → KEEP (0.9) — “CTA again demonstrates a large L-sided anterior aneurysm.” Based on
     bleeding distribution and location, this ‘anterior’ aneurysm is functionally equivalent to left MCA territory involvement.


     Attending Arbitration: The neurosurgeon and vascular neurologist provide converging anatomical reasoning that
     “L-sided anterior” maps to MCA territory given the hemorrhage distribution. The neurointensivist’s objection reflects
     strict labeling criteria but does not provide counter-evidence against MCA localization. → INCLUDE.


     Cerebral edema with compression
     C ONSENSUS : K EEP
     Neurosurgeon → KEEP (1.0) — “Increase in midline shift to approximately 11 mm.” Severe midline shift is direct
     evidence of mass effect and cerebral edema causing compression.
     Neurointensivist → KEEP (1.0) — “Newly blown right pupil with left pupil 2 mm and fixed.” The abnormal pupillary
     changes (herniation signs) confirm neurologic decline from compressive edema.
     Vascular Neurologist → KEEP (0.9) — “Loaded with mannitol and transferred.” Clinical use of mannitol protocol
     corroborates the presence of severe compressive edema.


     Vestibular migraine
     C ONSENSUS : R EMOVE (Distractor)
     Neurosurgeon → REMOVE (1.0) — No mention of migraine history; current herniation signs preclude this diagnosis.
     Neurointensivist → REMOVE (1.0) — GCS 3T and intubated status are incompatible with vestibular clinical presenta-
     tion.
     Vascular Neurologist → REMOVE (1.0) — “GCS 3T with absent cough and gag reflexes.” Severity of acute neurological
     collapse is inconsistent with the benign nature of migraine.


    Final Output: {Cerebral edema with compression, Left MCA aneurysm, Subarachnoid hemorrhage}.

Case 10330554-DS-13:
Gait Instability with Vascular and Alcohol Etiologies

Case Summary. A 65-year-old male with a history of HTN, DM, and chronic ETOH abuse presents with acute unsteadiness.
The patient admits to vodka consumption at 12 AM and 4 AM. Exam reveals a wide-based gait with stumbling to the
right. Imaging shows nonvisualization of the right vertebral artery and focal left ICA stenosis. The diagnostic focus is
differentiating between ethanol-induced ataxia and posterior circulation insufficiency.
Gold Diagnoses: Gait difficulty, likely related to alcohol use, Right vertebral artery occlusion, Left
ICA stenosis.


  Gait difficulty, likely related to alcohol use
  C ONFLICT → A RBITRATION : ACCEPT
  Neurologist → KEEP (0.7) — “Gait: Wide based, stumbles to the right.” While the right-sided stumbling is focal, the
  wide-based gait in the setting of active ETOH intake strongly suggests ethanol-related cerebellar impact.
  Vascular Surgeon → REMOVE (0.6) — “Nonvisualization of the right vertebral artery. . . likely due to occlusion.” The
  patient’s specific tendency to ‘stumble to the right’ aligns anatomically with the right vertebral artery pathology rather
  than systemic intoxication.
  Addiction Specialist → KEEP (0.9) — “He awoke around 4 am. . . each time he had a drink or two of vodka.” Active
  consumption less than one hour prior to symptoms directly correlates with the onset of acute ataxia.


  Attending Arbitration: The temporal correlation between acute alcohol consumption and symptom onset is the
  strongest evidence. The vascular surgeon’s lateralization argument, while plausible, is lower-confidence (0.6) and
  speculative. The neurologist and addiction specialist converge on the ethanol etiology. → INCLUDE.


  Right vertebral artery occlusion
  C ONSENSUS : K EEP
  Neurologist → KEEP (0.8) — “CTA HEAD/NECK: Nonvisualization of the right vertebral artery from its origin.”
  Imaging evidence supports occlusion; although MRI showed no acute infarct, chronic occlusion impacts global
  perfusion.
  Vascular Surgeon → KEEP (1.0) — “Minimal retrograde flow into the V4 segment of the right vertebral artery.”
  Nonvisualization at the origin with distal retrograde flow is pathognomonic for total occlusion; requires hemodynamic
  assessment.
  Addiction Specialist → NEUTRAL (0.0) — Interpretation of complex neurovascular flow patterns is outside the scope
  of addiction medicine expertise.


  Left ICA stenosis
  C ONSENSUS : K EEP
  Neurologist → KEEP (0.8) — “Focal narrowing of the distal ICA just before the entrance into the carotid canal.”
  Significant narrowing identifies a high-risk vascular profile, necessitating secondary stroke prevention.
  Vascular Surgeon → KEEP (0.9) — “Focal narrowing. . . consideration should be given to dissection.” Critical
  segment narrowing requires MRI/MRA to rule out acute dissection, which would fundamentally change the surgical
  management.
  Addiction Specialist → NEUTRAL (0.0) — Large vessel stenosis is a structural vascular diagnosis and does not impact
  the immediate detoxification or substance abuse treatment plan.


  Left frontal SDH
  C ONSENSUS : R EMOVE (Distractor)
  Neurologist → REMOVE (1.0) — “CT HEAD: No evidence of acute intracranial process.” Acute hemorrhage is
  radiographically excluded.
  Vascular Surgeon → REMOVE (1.0) — No vascular or traumatic evidence to support a subdural collection.
  Addiction Specialist → REMOVE (1.0) — Clinical presentation is more consistent with ETOH effects than trauma-
  induced mass effect.


Final Output: {Gait difficulty, likely related to alcohol use, Left ICA stenosis, Right vertebral
artery occlusion}.
