ABSTRACT
                                                               ES
                                                        PR
Electronic health records (EHRs) are a rich source of real-world clinical data, but turning them
into valid analyses remains slow, brittle, and expert-intensive. Although recent AI agents can
                                                   IN
answer medical questions and use tools, automating full EHR workflows remains difficult because
small planning or execution errors can invalidate otherwise plausible analyses. Here we present
HealthFlow, a multi-agent framework that converts prior EHR analyses into structured, governed
                                        LE
experience for planning under dataset-specific and methodological constraints. We also introduce
EHRFlowBench, a rigorously curated benchmark of EHR analysis tasks derived from 51,280
                                    C
peer-reviewed papers. Across EHRFlowBench and four established benchmarks (MedAgentBoard,
                              TI
MedAgentsBench, HLE, and CureBench), HealthFlow consistently outperforms strong baselines
in generating valid clinical artifacts and completing complex EHR analysis pipelines. These results
                           R
show that governed reuse of prior analytical experience improves robustness in health data science
                       A
and opens a promising path to automating open-ended EHR analysis.



1 Introduction
The digitization of healthcare has generated massive repositories of electronic health records
(EHRs) and an exponentially growing body of scientific literature1, 2 . While these resources hold
substantial promise for precision medicine and clinical research, transforming high-dimensional,
heterogeneous clinical data into actionable insights remains a persistent challenge: EHR analysis is
constrained by data-quality variation, documentation artifacts, and methodological validity risks3–7 .
Traditionally, this process relies on human experts, making it labor-intensive, difficult to scale, and
sensitive to analyst variability. Recent Large Language Models (LLMs) and LLM-driven agentic
systems have demonstrated the potential to automate parts of this workflow by orchestrating code
generation, tool usage, and retrieval over biomedical resources8–15 . Extending these capabilities
to EHR analysis requires end-to-end workflow orchestration, spanning cohort definition, feature
construction, modeling, visualization, and reporting to turn heterogeneous patient records into
reproducible analytical outputs.
    Recent agent research has expanded planning, tool use, and reflection across general and biomedi-
                                     ARTICLE IN PRESS




cal settings, yet much of this progress relies on fixed control structures or narrowly scoped refinement
mechanisms. Early general-purpose systems such as AutoGPT16 , GPT4Tools17 , HuggingGPT18 , and
MetaGPT19 broadened tool-augmented planning and execution in open domains, while scientific and
biomedical systems such as SciToolAgent20 , Robin21 , Biomni22 , BioMedAgent23 , and EvoMDT24
extended these ideas to research and medicine. Methods including Reflexion25 , Self-Refine26 ,
TextGrad27 , and AlphaEvolve28 sharpen outputs through feedback or code optimization, while
BioDSA29 , AFlow30 , Alita31 , and STELLA32 expand plan-centric scaffolding, workflow generation,
tools, or reusable reasoning structures. These advances broaden what agents can do but do not
resolve how accumulated experience should be carried forward under explicit validity constraints in
high-stakes EHR analysis.
    The central unresolved problem is governed reuse of prior experience: how prior experience
should be represented, bounded, and retrieved so that useful strategies inform later tasks without
propagating invalid assumptions33, 34 . EHR analysis is particularly demanding for this problem
because success depends not only on producing code or invoking tools but on maintaining method-
                                                                    S
ological validity across heterogeneous datasets, open-ended tasks, and partial failures. Failure is
                                                             ES
often not an obvious crash but a plausible analysis with hidden flaws, including temporal leakage,
invalid patient-level splitting, unsafe handling of missingness, or clinically implausible aggregation.
                                                      PR
    To address this gap, we introduce HealthFlow, a strategically self-evolving multi-agent framework
for autonomous EHR analysis. HealthFlow organizes completed analyses into structured, inspectable
                                              IN
forms of knowledge: methodological safeguards, reusable multi-step workflows, stable dataset-
grounded facts, and implementation guidance. These memories are written back, retrieved, and
updated over time under governed conditions tied to task family, dataset signature, schema cues,
                                      LE
and risk tags. By selectively reusing accumulated experience under explicit validity constraints,
HealthFlow ensures that future planning recovers not only what previously worked, but under what
                                  C
clinical and structural parameters it remains applicable.
                             TI
    Existing evaluation benchmarks are insufficient for assessing autonomous EHR analysis agents.
                         R
Datasets such as MedQA35 or PubMedQA36 assess medical knowledge through multiple-choice
                      A
questions, but exam-style settings do not test data handling, workflow construction, artifact verifi-
cation, or recovery from implementation errors37, 38 . General agent benchmarks such as GAIA39
evaluate broad tool use, multi-step reasoning, and web navigation, but do not capture the domain-
specific data handling, artifact validation, and workflow fidelity required for EHR analysis38, 40, 41 .
We therefore introduce EHRFlowBench, a rigorously curated benchmark of 100 EHR analysis
tasks derived from a large-scale screen of 51,280 peer-reviewed papers and finalized through expert
adjudication, requiring agents to perform full-cycle analysis from cohort definition and feature
engineering to statistical modeling and reporting.
    We demonstrate that HealthFlow consistently outperforms state-of-the-art frameworks across five
benchmarks spanning open-ended EHR workflow orchestration, EHR data analysis and modeling,
medical knowledge reasoning, and tool-augmented biomedical decision-making: EHRFlowBench,
MedAgentBoard42 , MedAgentsBench43 , HLE44 , and CureBench45 . These results show the value of
governed experience reuse for improving robustness in autonomous EHR analysis and accelerating
data-driven healthcare research.

2 Results
2.1 Overview of the HealthFlow Framework
HealthFlow (Figure 1) is designed for EHR analysis settings in which heterogeneous schemas, open-
ended workflows, and risks such as temporal leakage, invalid patient-level splitting, unsafe missing-
value handling, and clinically implausible aggregation can invalidate otherwise plausible outputs.
Together, Figure 1a,b define a closed-loop architecture for controlling this failure mode: Figure 1a
summarizes end-to-end coordination across planning, execution, evaluation, and reflection; the
EHR-aware task context and governed experience memory modules are expanded in Supplementary
Figure 1a,b; and Figure 1b separates immediate repair from cross-task memory writeback, updating,
and retirement, with the executor operating through an external coding-agent backend and extensible
                                     ARTICLE IN PRESS




command-line interface (CLI)- and Model Context Protocol (MCP)46 -based tool support. This design
is intended to make experience reuse useful without allowing invalid assumptions to propagate across
heterogeneous EHR settings. In the benchmark view, Figure 1c shows that HealthFlow variants
outperform representative general and biomedical agents on EHRFlowBench, and Figure 1d shows
that this comparison is drawn from a 100-task benchmark spanning 10 EHR-analysis categories,
including temporal prediction, representation and feature engineering, phenotyping and clustering,
robustness and missingness analysis, etc.

2.2 HealthFlow Consistently Outperforms Baselines Across Five Benchmarks
We evaluated HealthFlow on five benchmarks spanning open-ended EHR workflow orchestration,
executable EHR analysis, medical knowledge reasoning, and tool-augmented decision-making. Our
primary testbed for open-ended EHR analysis is EHRFlowBench, instantiated on TJH and the
MIMIC-IV Public Demo Release47, 48 . EHRFlowBench comprises 100 tasks derived from 118 peer-
reviewed seed papers and evaluates the ability to translate clinical objectives into concrete analytical
                                                                   S
pipelines, including cohort construction, feature engineering, modeling, visualization, and report
                                                             ES
writing, across 10 task categories summarized in Figure 1d. We additionally used a workflow-focused
executable task set based on MedAgentBoard42 covering data extraction, predictive modeling, and
                                                      PR
visualization, also instantiated on TJH and the MIMIC-IV Public Demo Release. We further
evaluated medical knowledge and reasoning using three deterministic multiple-choice benchmarks:
the MedAgentsBench hard set43 , selected biology and medicine questions from Humanity’s Last
                                              IN
Exam (HLE)44 , and selected questions from CureBench45 (100 randomly selected questions from
each benchmark).
    Across this evaluation suite, HealthFlow achieved the highest score on all five benchmarks
                                      LE
(Figure 2a). The clearest separation appeared on the two workflow-intensive benchmarks that
                                  C
most closely match the target use case. On EHRFlowBench, HealthFlow reached 4.01, compared
with 3.76 for the strongest baseline, Biomni22 . On MedAgentBoard, it achieved 63.05% success,
                             TI
compared with 50.10% for Biomni, a 12.95-percentage-point difference. HealthFlow also led the
                         R
three more knowledge-oriented benchmarks, topping MedAgentsBench, HLE, and CureBench.
                      A
    Head-to-head comparisons on EHRFlowBench (Figure 2c) show that the aggregate gains are
distributed across tasks rather than driven by a small number of outliers. HealthFlow outscored
AFlow, Alita, BioMedAgent, and STELLA on 95.0–98.0% of tasks. The comparisons were tighter
against BioDSA and Biomni, but the HealthFlow-higher category still remained the largest seg-
ment in both cases (52.0% and 38.0%, respectively). In our runs, much of the gap came from
incomplete long-horizon workflows, especially missing or partial reports and artifacts, while Biomni
and BioDSA remained closer but still lagged on full EHR-specific protocol completion. These
distributions suggest that the main bottleneck in autonomous health data science is not only access
to medical knowledge, but the reliable orchestration of that knowledge into valid end-to-end an-
alytical pipelines. The same pattern appears in blinded human evaluation on randomly selected
EHRFlowBench reports (Figure 2e), which we analyze separately below.

EHR-aware planner and evaluator, the self-evolving reflector with governed memory retrieval and
synthesis, and finally task-matched external tools through OneEHR and ToolUniverse. Performance
improved monotonically as components were added. Relative to the executor-only reference, the

2.5 Case Study of HealthFlow on a Representative EHRFlowBench Task
To show how HealthFlow handles an open-ended EHRFlowBench task, we examine a predictive-
modeling case centered on representation learning under strict split-preservation and leakage-control
requirements (Figure 4). This task uses the MIMIC-IV Public Demo Release and asks the agent
to predict outcome from raw, flat-cluster, and hierarchical masked representations, preserve the
provided train/validation/test partitions, audit negative-pair removal, and deliver tables, figures,
                                                                   S
and a report. The difficulty is therefore not merely generating runnable code, but maintaining
                                                             ES
methodological validity when apparently strong metrics can arise from invalid preprocessing or split
handling.
                                                      PR
    In the first attempt, the meta agent drafts a seemingly reasonable predictive-modeling pipeline,
but the executor returns anomalously perfect test AUROC and AUPRC together with an implausibly
low validation loss on a split-preserved EHR task (Figure 4a). HealthFlow’s evaluator does not
                                              IN
treat this as a benign high score; instead, it interprets the jointly unrealistic discrimination and
validation behavior as a structured EHR risk signal consistent with leakage or split misuse and
therefore triggers a leakage-focused review. This is the within-task repair mechanism described
                                      LE
in Figure 1b at full resolution: once the task profile marks the case as predictive modeling with
                                  C
high-severity target leakage, evaluator feedback is folded into the next attempt as explicit
repair guidance rather than as unconstrained reflective text. The planner then updates the next plan to
                             TI
enforce train-only imputation and scaling, train-only hierarchical clustering, validation-only tuning
                         R
of the masking quantile, and strict preservation of the provided splits before rerunning the pipeline.
                      A
    The same trajectory also illustrates how HealthFlow resolves potential memory conflict through
governed retrieval rather than by exposing the planner to a large undifferentiated memory bank.
After the evaluator flags leakage, bounded per-type retrieval admits only two risk-matched safe-
guards, ehr modeling pipeline and ehr predictive modeling pipeline, into the
planning context (Figure 4a). The practical effect is that memory becomes an execution constraint:
guidance relevant to the active leakage risk is promoted, while less relevant procedural advice
does not compete with the repair. In the repaired run, the executor rebuilds the workflow from
scratch, completes data loading and integrity checks, engineers 121 features across 17 channels, and
produces a more plausible hierarchical-masking result with an AUPRC of 0.8333. The evaluator
then confirms that all required artifacts are generated and that train-only preprocessing removes the
principal methodological concern.
    The final outputs in Figure 4b,c make the same distinction visible at the artifact level. HealthFlow
delivers the requested representation-wise comparison together with the hierarchical-clustering
dendrogram, which provides direct evidence that the full raw-versus-flat-versus-hierarchical protocol
was actually executed. By contrast, the final figures from BioDSA29 and Biomni22 summarize
narrower views of performance and do not surface the full protocol requested by the task, leaving
the underlying analytical path less transparent. After success, the reflector writes back a leakage-
prevention safeguard, a reusable workflow for this representation-learning pattern, and code-level

2.6 Blinded Expert Evaluation and Practical Utility of HealthFlow
We additionally conducted a blinded expert evaluation to assess the practical utility of the gen-
erated solutions. Twelve domain experts independently reviewed the same set of 20 randomly
selected EHRFlowBench reports from the 100-task benchmark, and selected the best response under
anonymized presentation. As summarized in Figure 2e, HealthFlow received the largest vote share
on the broadest set of tasks. Because evaluators selected a single best complete report, closely
matched tasks naturally produced split votes. Even so, HealthFlow led most often, while BioDSA,
Biomni, and BioMedAgent remained competitive on a smaller subset of tasks. This practical-utility
signal aligns with our qualitative feedback from a preliminary offline hospital pilot deployment
in nephrology and obstetrics, where clinicians reported that HealthFlow-assisted EHR analysis
                                                                    S
workflows could be shortened from several hours to tens of minutes; the public system is available
at https://healthflow.medx-pku.com/app.                      ES
3 Discussion                                           PR
Our results place methodological validity at the center of autonomous EHR analysis. In this
                                              IN
setting, failure often takes the form of “plausible invalidity”, where an analysis appears coherent
while violating leakage control, patient-level splitting, aggregation logic, or protocol constraints.
HealthFlow performs well because the full control loop is grounded in dataset structure, risk tags,
                                      LE
and prior failure evidence, so analytical intent is translated into executable workflows with tighter
                                   C
methodological discipline. This point is important for EHR automation: many errors emerge during
orchestration, not at the level of medical facts alone.
                             TI
    The ablation, runtime, and retrieval analyses also clarify what strategic self-evolution contributes.
                          R
Performance improves when experience is reorganized into forms that match recurring analytical de-
                      A
cisions. Open-ended studies repeatedly benefit from safeguards, whereas more structured workflows
draw on a broader mix of workflows, dataset anchors, and code snippets. Planner-evaluator control
yields the largest gains even though execution dominates cost, which indicates that search-space
shaping, risk diagnosis, and structured recovery have higher leverage than raw execution throughput.
Taken together, these patterns argue for evaluating medical agents in workflow settings that expose
implementation, recovery, and adaptation over time.
    Several limitations remain. As experience accumulates, maintaining memory quality and
resolving conflicts may become harder across institutions with different schemas, coding conventions,
and outcome definitions. More broadly, a continually growing memory bank introduces a scalability
trade-off: stale but high-confidence memories may crowd out newer institution-specific guidance,
whereas overly aggressive retirement or confidence decay may discard rare but clinically important
safeguards, creating a workflow-level analogue of catastrophic forgetting. Future work should
therefore study memory compression, hierarchical indexing, recency-aware confidence updates, and
institution-specific partitioning so that long-horizon reuse remains stable as the task count grows.
Public, de-identified datasets and rubric-based LLM judging also do not capture the operational,
privacy, and safety constraints of deployment, so more prospective, multi-center studies are needed.
Expert oversight remains necessary for confounding assessment, causal interpretation, and judgments
of clinical meaningfulness.

    In conclusion, HealthFlow shows that strategically self-evolving agents have the potential to
make autonomous clinical data science more reliable by turning past analytical trajectories into
structured, inspectable evidence with explicit scope, update rules, and retirement criteria. This
design helps bridge raw electronic health records and actionable clinical insights by supporting
cohort construction, feature engineering, modeling, and reporting across open-ended health-data
workflows. Extending this framework to richer hospital tool ecosystems, multimodal data, and
larger institutional environments will clarify how far such systems can support rigorous healthcare
discovery.

                              R

                          A

4.5 Evaluation Protocol
To rigorously assess agent performance across the diverse requirements of health data science,
we employ a hybrid evaluation strategy combining deterministic metrics, reference-guided LLM
judging, and expert human review. For the three knowledge-intensive deterministic multiple-choice
benchmarks, namely the MedAgentsBench43 hard set and the selected HLE44 and CureBench45
subsets, we report accuracy after normalizing each prediction to a single answer choice. In contrast,
EHRFlowBench and MedAgentBoard42 require artifact-level evaluation because a single short
answer is insufficient to capture methodological validity.
    For artifact-generation tasks on EHRFlowBench and MedAgentBoard42 , task extraction, ref-
erence construction, agent execution, and judging are decoupled to mitigate self-preference and
closed-loop bias. Task extraction was supported by OpenAI GPT-5.467 and subsequently curated by
human reviewers, whereas artifact evaluation used reference answers prepared by human experts
and independently cross-validated by separate domain experts. Agent execution used models from
the DeepSeek and Qwen families, and judging was conducted with Google Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite68 .
Specifically, on EHRFlowBench, each agent-generated markdown report is rendered to PDF and
                                    ARTICLE IN PRESS




compared against the reference-answer PDF using Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite68 . The judge scores
methodology soundness, presentation quality, and artifact generation on a 1-to-5 scale; tasks that
fail to produce the required artifacts receive a score of 0. For variants with evaluator feedback, we
evaluate the first attempt that meets the evaluator’s success threshold. If none does, we evaluate the
third-attempt artifacts when available; if required assets such as the report are missing, the task is
scored 0. MedAgentBoard42 is designed to be as deterministic as possible: data tasks are judged
against exact or semantically equivalent structured outputs, predictive-modeling tasks are judged
against the corresponding reference artifacts while allowing approximately 10% metric deviation
when the reported model behavior remains materially consistent with the ground truth, and visual-
ization tasks are counted as correct when they faithfully express the requested analytical meaning
even if stylistic details or chart types differ. These task-specific judgments on MedAgentBoard42 are
likewise implemented with Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite68 as a binary success metric (0 for failure and 1
for success). Agreement analysis in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Figure 2a,b) shows
that Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite closely tracks the stronger judge and the expert labels: Supplementary
                                                                  S

                                                           ES

expert ratings on MedAgentBoard. Together, these results support Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite as a

DeepSeek-V3.2-Think53, 54 , or Qwen3.5-Flash62 ; the backend model was either DeepSeek-V3.253 ,
DeepSeek-V3.2-Think53, 54 , or Qwen3.5-Flash62 when OpenCode was used; and Claude Code63 and
Pi64 were each paired with DeepSeek-V3.253 as the backend model. Within the self-correction loop,
HealthFlow allowed up to 3 full attempts per task, used an evaluator success threshold of 0.8, and
set the retrieval budgets to K = 6 and Ktype = 2. In the experiments, OneEHR51 and ToolUniverse52
were connected through command-line interfaces. We additionally recorded stage-wise token usage
and estimated cost for planning, execution, evaluation, and reflection. The reported stage-wise cost
totals use this reference configuration and are accumulated over 100 tasks per benchmark. This setup
isolates the contribution of HealthFlow’s EHR-aware harness, strategically self-evolving memory
mechanisms, and tool-use layer from the standalone capability of the executor backend.
