Abstract


Large language models (LLMs) show promise in emergency medicine, however, their role in emergency neurology


remains unclear. We developed a customized LLM, Xuanwu-NeuroAid, and prospectively enrolled 433 patients. The


diagnostic outputs of the model and emergency physicians were compared with confirmed diagnoses, and an expert


panel performed blinded evaluations of the recommendations generated by both the model and physicians. The


independent diagnostic accuracy of the model was 79.4%, which was significantly higher than that of emergency

                                                                              S
physicians (65.4%, P < 0.001). Blinded expert assessments indicated that the model’s recommendations for
                                                                      ES
examinations and treatments were significantly more comprehensive, accurate, and clinically applicable than those of
                                                              PR
physicians (P < 0.001). Moreover, the inclusion of demographic information altered the model’s recommendations for

                                                     IN
health education, suggesting a sensitivity to sociodemographic factors. Our findings highlight the potential of the LLM

                                            LE
to enhance diagnostic precision and support decision-making in emergency neurology under simulated clinical

                                       C
conditions. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06779292; January 6, 2025).

Introduction


Emergency departments (EDs) serve as central hubs for managing acute and critical illnesses in hospitals, and the


demand for emergency services has steadily increased in recent years. However, investigations into ED operations


have revealed that although patient visits continue to increase annually, the rate of hospital admissions from the ED


has shown a downward trend, indicating limitations in hospital capacity for admission and care 1. Meanwhile, previous


studies have reported that medical errors are the third leading cause of death in the United States 2. In emergency

                                                                               S
settings, physicians are often required to evaluate multiple patients simultaneously, frequently with incomplete clinical
                                                                       ES
information, making the ED a high-risk environment for adverse events3. Epidemiological data further show that 30-
                                                               PR
day revisit rates after ED encounters are as high as 19.9%, with the cost of revisits often exceeding that of the initial

                                                      IN
visit4. These issues not only add to the healthcare burden but also contribute to patient dissatisfaction and complaints.

                                            LE
Neurological diseases constitute a substantial proportion of ED visits. Conditions such as acute ischemic stroke and

                                        C
seizure are time-sensitive, and rapid, accurate diagnosis and treatment are crucial to improving prognosis and reducing
                                  TI
                              R
mortality5. However, studies have indicated that the misdiagnosis rate of acute stroke can range from 4% to 64%
                         A
across different populations, which is substantially higher than that observed in cardiovascular emergencies6,7.


Diagnostic errors in cerebrovascular events are strongly associated with markedly increased mortality (45% vs. 1%)8.


Moreover, even among patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis, 1.8%-21.0% are ultimately confirmed to be stroke


mimics9-11, underscoring the complexity and challenges of diagnosing neurological emergencies in the ED. Therefore,


the management of neurological emergencies requires not only speed but also a high level of diagnostic precision.


Against this backdrop, the development of intelligent systems capable of assisting physicians with real-time diagnoses


and promptly correcting potential misjudgments has become an urgent requirement to enhance the efficiency and


accuracy of emergency care.
                                           ARTICLE IN PRESS




Potential applications of deep learning in neurology have attracted significant attention. With architectures, including


convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and transformers, deep learning models


can efficiently process medical imaging, clinical data, and multimodal information, thereby markedly improving both


the accuracy and efficiency of diagnosis12,13. In clinical practice, machine learning models have been applied to stroke


recognition, automated neuroimaging analysis, prediction of stroke recurrence risk, and the auxiliary diagnosis of


diseases such as Parkinson’s disease14-17. However, existing models still have limitations, including insufficient

                                                                              S
generalization ability, strong dependence on high-quality training data17,18, and high computational resource
                                                                       ES
consumption14. These constraints are particularly pronounced in real-world medical settings, and limit generalization


and clinical translation.
                                                               PR
                                                     IN
In recent years, large language models (LLMs), with their exceptional semantic understanding and generative

                                            LE
capabilities19,20, have been integrated into the medical domain, driving the advancement of diagnostic and therapeutic

                                           C
processes toward intelligence and automation. Building on transformer architectures, LLMs can integrate patient
                                 TI
                             R
complaints, vital signs, and examination results to perform logical reasoning, thereby demonstrating strong potential
                            A
in disease recognition, clinical assessment, and therapeutic guidance21. With the expansion of model parameters and


the systematic training of medical-specific corpora, LLMs have achieved excellent performance in standardized


tasks22,23. However, most current studies remain limited to structured inputs and static testing environments. In


complicated clinical environments such as emergency neurology—characterized by limited information availability,


restricted communication, and a high risk of misdiagnosis—the adaptability and clinical applicability of LLMs have


not yet been systematically validated24.


In this context, we present Xuanwu-NeuroAid, a medical LLM that simulates the clinical reasoning process of


physicians across the stages of “listening–thinking–judging–decision.” This study represents an early-stage, non-
                                          ARTICLE IN PRESS




interventional evaluation of the model’s diagnostic performance and its potential role as a decision-support system in


emergency neurology.     Three hypotheses were tested: the first was to assess the accuracy of the initial diagnoses


generated by the model; the second was to evaluate the appropriateness of its recommendations for examinations and


treatments; and the third was to explore whether including patient demographic information alters the examination


and treatment suggestions produced by the model.      We designed a structured therapeutic framework encompassing


the entire clinical workflow, including consultation input, diagnostic generation, examination and treatment


recommendations, and blinded expert evaluation.                               S
                                                       We conducted a multidimensional validation of the model's
                                                                      ES
performance by assessing the consistency among model diagnoses, physicians’ initial diagnoses, AI-assisted diagnoses,
                                                              PR
and final confirmed diagnoses, combined with expert scoring of recommendations.

                                                     IN
Unlike previous LLM studies that primarily focused on static tasks such as medical question answering, this study

                                            LE
explores the model’s performance characteristics within a simulated emergency department workflow under high-

                                       C
pressure clinical conditions. Using real-world patient data in a shadow-mode evaluation, we aimed to explore the
                                 TI
                             R
feasibility of model assessment and methodological validity. Ultimately, it provides a replicable methodological
                         A
framework and a practical foundation for the future clinical translation of intelligent diagnostic systems in emergency


care.

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics


This study enrolled 650 patients who presented to the Department of Emergency Neurology. After the assessment, 217


patients were excluded from the study. Of these, 177 had incomplete medical records for a definitive diagnosis, 27


were referred to other departments, 3 had unstable vital signs, and 10 had repeat visits. The enrollment process is


shown in Figure 2. In total, 433 patients were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the patients was 61.3 ±
                                          ARTICLE IN PRESS




15.9 years, and 52.7% were male. Education levels were distributed as follows: primary school, 58 patients (13.4%);


middle school, 133 patients (30.7%); high school, 165 patients (38.1%); and college or above, 77 patients (17.8%).


Insurance coverage was as follows: 201 patients (46.4%) were enrolled in urban employee basic health insurance, 129


(29.8%) in urban resident basic health insurance, 102 (23.6%) in new rural cooperative medical insurance, and 1


patient (0.2%) had no insurance. All enrolled patients provided complete encounter recordings, ancillary test results,


and physical examination data. Baseline demographic and clinical variables were standardized prior to analysis to


ensure the validity and consistency of subsequent comparisons (Table 1).     S

Discussion

                                                                              S
Based on data from 433 real-world emergency neurology patients evaluated in a shadow-mode setting, this study
                                                                      ES
systematically assessed the diagnostic performance and recommendation characteristics of the customized LLM
                                                              PR
Xuanwu-NeuroAid under simulated clinical conditions. The results showed that the model achieved higher

                                                     IN
independent diagnostic accuracy than emergency physicians, and physicians’ diagnostic accuracy markedly improved

                                            LE
when assisted by the model. These findings suggest that the model demonstrates potential advantages in diagnosis and

                                       C
may support decision-making processes. Further analysis revealed that the comprehensiveness, accuracy, and clinical
                                 TI
                             R
applicability of the model’s recommendations for examinations and treatments were significantly superior to those of
                         A
physicians. However, these assessments reflect expert judgments under controlled evaluation conditions and do not


directly measure clinical impact or patient benefit. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in


the recommendations for examinations, treatments, or rehabilitation before and after the inclusion of demographic


information, the recommendation rate for health education differed following the addition of educational level and


insurance information. This suggests that the model may be more sensitive to sociodemographic characteristics when


generating recommendations related to patient cognition and adherence. Collectively, these findings suggest that the


LLM demonstrates promising performance characteristics in a simulated emergency neurology workflow.


In recent years, the complexity of emergency medical environments has increased, requiring physicians to make
                                           ARTICLE IN PRESS




critical decisions under time constraints and with incomplete information—conditions that impose strict demands on


diagnostic efficiency and accuracy25,26. Traditional intelligent support systems, which depend on structured data and


rule-based algorithms, often struggle to process the heterogeneous, multimodal, and context-dependent information


in the emergency department27,28. In contrast, LLMs equipped with advanced semantic understanding and generative


capabilities have demonstrated considerable potential in tasks such as medical question answering and clinical


summarization29,30. Nevertheless, existing LLMs used in medical applications remain limited by several factors,

                                                                                S
including inherent biases in training data, incomplete medical knowledge coverage, inadequate adherence to real-
                                                                        ES
world clinical workflows and guidelines, and suboptimal adaptability to complex clinical contexts. These
                                                                PR
shortcomings raise legitimate concerns about the fairness, accuracy, and practical usability of LLMs in medical

                                                      IN
decision-making31-33. In this study, Xuanwu-NeuroAid incorporating scenario-specific training and optimized

                                             LE
reasoning mechanisms, was evaluated within a structured, simulated emergency neurology workflow under a shadow-

                                        C
mode setting. The system was assessed on its ability in diagnostic generation, decision support, and therapeutic
                                  TI
                              R
recommendations, rather than to influence real-time clinical care. This framework provides an initial methodological
                          A
exploration of how domain-specific LLMs may support complex clinical reasoning tasks. However, the


generalizability and cross-disciplinary applicability of such systems still require validation in future prospective real-


world implementation studies.


From a methodological perspective, Xuanwu-NeuroAid integrates multiple innovative designs to enhance its


practicality and reasoning reliability under simulated emergency care conditions. By employing structured pruning


and parameter-sharing mechanisms, the model preserves the core performance of a 70B-parameter architecture while


reducing computational costs to levels compatible with standard GPUs. It supports long-text inputs of up to 6,144


tokens, enabling the comprehensive integration of medical history, physical findings, and examination results in a
                                           ARTICLE IN PRESS




single pass, thereby avoiding information loss caused by truncation in conventional models. During training,


reinforcement learning with the GRPO algorithm was applied to systematically rank and optimize outputs based on


logical completeness, consistency, and misdiagnosis risk, thereby reducing the misdiagnosis rate from 13% to 8.6%.


A high-freeze LoRA fine-tuning strategy was employed to rapidly adapt the model to neurology-specific tasks using


a limited set of high-quality dialogues. Beyond diagnostic accuracy, explainability remains a major barrier to the


clinical adoption of artificial intelligence. Traditional approaches, such as post hoc feature attribution methods, mainly

                                                                                S
provide mathematical interpretations of model behavior but often fail to align with physicians’ clinical reasoning34.
                                                                        ES
Previous studies have shown that these methods have limited practical value in real-world clinical settings, as
                                                                PR
clinicians require explanations that are clinically interpretable, contextually relevant, and decision-guiding. Clinician-

                                                      IN
oriented explanations have been demonstrated to influence clinical decision making more effectively than

                                             LE
conventional attribution-based approaches35, and recent non-randomized controlled studies further highlight the

                                        C
importance of establishing clinically meaningful associations in real-world applications36. In this context, Xuanwu-
                                  TI
                              R
NeuroAid adopts an explicit chain-of-reasoning framework that structures the diagnostic process into symptom
                          A
analysis, differential diagnosis, examination recommendations, and conclusions. This design not only enhances


reasoning transparency but also provides clinicians with a structured and traceable pathway that more closely reflects


clinical thinking. Additional components, including temperature calibration and attention-based uncertainty


quantification, further support the generation of transparent and interpretable diagnostic recommendations. Compared


with traditional post hoc methods, this process-level transparency may offer more intuitive and clinically meaningful


explanations. However, whether such an approach can improve trust, usability, and clinical outcomes remains to be


validated in future prospective human–AI interaction studies.


The results of this study show that Xuanwu-NeuroAid achieved higher diagnostic accuracy than physicians and that
                                          ARTICLE IN PRESS




physician performance also improved with model assistance. Under the LLM-assisted mode, the physicians’


diagnostic accuracy improved significantly from 65.4% to 75.1%, indicating that the model can effectively assist


physicians in identifying and correcting potential misdiagnoses through explicit reasoning chains and key diagnostic


clues, thereby optimizing the clinical decision-making process. Recent studies have raised concerns about the


performance of general-purpose LLMs in complex clinical reasoning. Their training paradigms are based mainly on


question–answer tasks and general medical knowledge, with limited adaptation to specialty-specific contexts and

                                                                             S
clinical workflows37,38. For example, an evaluation of GPT-4 using public case reports found that the concordance
                                                                     ES
between initial and final diagnoses was approximately 39%22. Another study showed that earlier models, such as
                                                              PR
Llama-2, performed worse than clinicians for common diseases31. In contrast, the LLM developed in this study

                                                    IN
incorporates long-context integration and explicit chain-of-thought reasoning, which improves diagnostic accuracy.

                                           LE
Its performance was assessed using real-world and unstructured clinical data, however, this evaluation was conducted

                                       C
in a shadow-mode setting and did not involve real-time clinical deployment. Notably, diagnostic performance differed
                                 TI
                             R
across disease subgroups. In the peripheral neuropathy subgroup, physicians showed higher diagnostic accuracy than
                         A
both the standalone model and the model assisted evaluation. This finding likely reflects both the complexity and


heterogeneity of peripheral nerve disorders, as well as the inherent limitations of the emergency setting. Essential


diagnostic information, including detailed neurological examinations, electrophysiological testing, and longitudinal


symptom evolution, is often unavailable at the time of emergency evaluation. In this context, clinicians may rely more


on bedside experience and clinical judgment, whereas the model is constrained by incomplete or limited inputs. This


result highlights an important limitation of human AI collaboration in complex or heterogeneous conditions and


suggests that AI based support should be applied with caution when early diagnostic information is limited. Overall,


these findings suggest that Xuanwu-NeuroAid exhibits promising performance characteristics in simulated emergency
                                           ARTICLE IN PRESS




neurology workflows. Its potential value is particularly pronounced in settings with limited human resources, such as


night shifts or primary care environments. Its real-world impact on physician behavior, diagnostic safety, and patient


outcomes remains to be determined through future prospective studies and randomized controlled trials.


Xuanwu-NeuroAid performed better than physicians in generating examinations and treatment recommendations.


Expert evaluations showed that the model outperformed physicians across all three dimensions—comprehensiveness,


accuracy, and clinical applicability (P < 0.001). Previous studies have shown that although most general-purpose

                                                                               S
LLMs are capable of responding to real-world health consultations, they often fail to provide concrete and actionable
                                                                       ES
management plans19 and lack detailed recommendations for common neurological diseases 39. By contrast, the
                                                               PR
specialized and context-oriented LLM developed in this study addresses these limitations in physicians’ clinical advice

                                                         IN
through standardized outputs. Overall, the model demonstrated promising performance in generating diagnostic,

                                            LE
examination, and treatment suggestions within a simulated emergency neurology workflow. However, these findings

                                        C
do not establish clinical effectiveness or workflow optimization in real-world practice, and the impact on resource
                                  TI
                              R
utilization and patient outcomes requires validation in future prospective studies. Notably, a small proportion of the
                         A
model’s recommendations (<10%) received a score of 3 or lower. Experts noted that these cases were mainly


concentrated in two scenarios: first, recommendations containing an excessive number of examinations, potentially


leading to unnecessary medical burdens; and second, drug treatment plans that lacked sufficient individualization,


failing to account for past medical history and potential comorbidities. These findings suggest that, in complex clinical


contexts, the model requires further optimization to strengthen its ability to generate personalized recommendations


and to improve its strategies for resource adaptation.


The inclusion of sociodemographic information led to a significant change in the rate of health education


recommendations generated by the LLM, whereas no statistically significant differences were found in the consistency
                                           ARTICLE IN PRESS




of recommendations for further examinations, therapeutic strategies, or rehabilitative interventions before and after


the addition of sociodemographic information. This finding suggests that the model may be more sensitive to


sociodemographic information when generating recommendations related to patient cognition and adherence.


Although neither univariate nor multivariate regression analyses identified significant independent associations among


educational level, type of medical insurance, or disease category and health education recommendations, the overall


trend observed after incorporating demographic information revealed the model’s potential sociological sensitivity.

                                                                               S
This observation is consistent with previous studies on social bias in LLMs, which have reported that such models
                                                                       ES
may amplify social biases in medical education, diagnosis, and treatment recommendations by relying on stereotypes
                                                               PR
associated with gender, race, or social identity32,40. Our findings further demonstrate that within a simulated emergency

                                                      IN
neurology workflow, the model may adjust certain recommendations based on sociodemographic information,

                                            LE
particularly in health education. Notably, this change is unlikely to result from the independent effect of any single

                                        C
demographic variable but may instead reflect social narrative patterns embedded within the model’s training corpus,
                                  TI
                              R
such as implicit associations linking educational and insurance status with patients perceived needs for health
                         A
education. From a clinical perspective, these tendencies present both opportunities and risks. On the one hand, tailoring


health education to differences in educational background and insurance coverage could enhance the specificity and


effectiveness of physician–patient communication. However, if the model amplifies social identity factors without


clinical justification, it may lead to unnecessary interventions for certain groups. Previous studies on LLM-related


biases have shown that generative LLMs may inherit and amplify social biases embedded in training data during large-


scale development and application. This process can systematically weaken the voices of marginalized groups and


further intensify the marginalization of their knowledge expression41. Therefore, physicians should remain vigilant


regarding potential sociodemographic biases when generating health education and management recommendations.
                                          ARTICLE IN PRESS




These findings suggest that the LLM is sensitive to sociodemographic factors when generating health education


recommendations. This observation underscores that when evaluating and applying LLMs in medical settings, it is


essential not only to examine diagnostic accuracy but also to thoroughly assess output differences across diverse


sociodemographic conditions. Future research should integrate real-world patient data and interdisciplinary


perspectives to develop strategies for identifying and mitigating social biases without compromising model


adaptability, thereby ensuring fair and accessible clinical implementation.

                                                                      ES
emergency neurology under simulated evaluation conditions. The architecture of Xuanwu-NeuroAid, which supports
                                                              PR

                                                     IN

                                        C

                                 TI

practice. Looking forward, the integration of vision–language models with imaging, video, and other multimodal
                         A

This study has some limitations. First, although based on prospectively collected emergency neurology cases, the


model was evaluated in a shadow-mode parallel setting rather than in a live clinical workflow. Its outputs did not


influence real-time decision-making, and key implementation factors such as time pressure, user interface constraints,


and cognitive load were not assessed. Second, to ensure reliable determination of confirmed diagnoses and to avoid


delays in clinical care, patients with incomplete clinical information or unstable vital signs were excluded. This

Model Development and Training Methods


This study established a natural language processing system for diagnostic support in emergency neurology, built on


the large language model Xuanwu-NeuroAid, and conducted a systematic design and validation focusing on model


architecture, training pipeline, inference mechanisms, and performance evaluation. Xuanwu-NeuroAid is a distilled


model derived from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B and comprises 70 billion parameters. Through structured

                                                                            S
pruning and parameter-sharing strategies, it achieved more than 90% of the original model’s task performance while
                                                                        ES
substantially reducing inference resource consumption, with costs approaching those of a 34B-level model. The model
                                                               PR
supports a maximum context length of 6,144 tokens, enabling the integration of complete medical histories, ancillary

                                                     IN
test results, and imaging reports across both structured and unstructured modalities in a single input, thereby

                                            LE
effectively mitigating information loss caused by text truncation. Within this long-context, language-based

                                        C
architecture, multimodal clinical information was integrated through a unified text representation. To accommodate
                                 TI
                             R
the time-sensitive and heterogeneous nature of emergency neurology workflows, this study adopted a text-based data
                         A
homogenization strategy for multimodal clinical information. Vital signs and structured laboratory results were


converted into standardized textual descriptors, while imaging information was incorporated through radiology report


text rather than raw image inputs. This approach unified heterogeneous data sources into a single natural language


sequence, allowing joint reasoning within the long-context framework of Xuanwu-NeuroAid. The primary advantages


of this text-centric integration include simplicity, low computational overhead, and minimal inference latency, which


are particularly important in emergency settings where rapid access to diagnostic support is required and structured


summaries of multimodal data are routinely available. However, this strategy does not constitute true end-to-end


multimodal modeling. It does not directly process raw imaging or structured signals, nor does it employ modality-
                                          ARTICLE IN PRESS




specific encoders or cross-modal alignment mechanisms. During inference, the integration of the VLLM KV-cache


warm-start mechanism with DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 distributed optimization ensured high-throughput deployment across


both training and inference phases. For engineering implementation, GPTQ-4bit quantization enabled eight concurrent


inference streams on a single A100 GPU with an average response latency of approximately one second, thereby


meeting the real-time requirements of clinical practice.


The training corpus comprised more than 310,000 multi-specialty physician–patient dialogues. Following semantic

                                                                             S
noise detection using the BERT model, regularized de-identification, and multi-turn Q&A rewriting, a final dataset of
                                                                     ES
90,000 high-quality Q&A pairs was established. Compared to traditional neurology datasets, this corpus is
                                                              PR
approximately three times larger, covering a broader range of comorbidity combinations and cross-disciplinary clinical

                                                     IN
scenarios, thereby providing a stronger foundation for model generalization in emergency neurology. All data

                                            LE
processing strictly adhered to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data

                                        C
Protection Regulation (GDPR) standards to ensure compliance and reusability.
                                 TI
                             R
To further improve generalization and performance on long-tail data, the study employed a “triplet” dataset—
                         A
comprising questions, original answers, and expert-corrected answers (Q-A-C)—curated by medical professionals.


Building on this foundation, we developed “Neuro-Aligner,” a lightweight alignment module designed to refine


responses by leveraging existing Q&A datasets, interdisciplinary clinical contexts, and domain-specific medical


terminology. This process enabled the creation of high-quality supervised fine-tuning datasets for long-tail


distributions while simultaneously providing preference datasets for reinforcement learning with human feedback


(RLHF), thereby supporting subsequent fine-tuning and reinforcement learning stages.


The training procedure began with supervised fine-tuning to align medical semantics, followed by the Group-Relative


Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm incorporating expert feedback. Traditional RLHF approaches model human
                                          ARTICLE IN PRESS




preferences through preference datasets, train reward models to assign high scores to appropriate responses, and apply


reinforcement learning algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to encourage outputs aligned with


human values. Instead of assigning rewards to a single sample, our method generated multiple candidate responses


within each symptom cluster and ranked them based on logical coherence, consistency with expert ratings, and


misdiagnosis penalty scores. This intra-group ranking and cross-step reward strategy effectively mitigated mode


collapse while enhancing reasoning diversity and safety. Preliminary experiments demonstrated that GRPO training

                                                                              S
reduced the misdiagnosis rate from 13% to 8.6%. During neurology-specific fine-tuning, we adopted a high-freeze
                                                                      ES
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) strategy, updating only the input embedding layer and selected intermediate layers.
                                                              PR
This approach enabled retraining with approximately 2,000–3,000 high-quality neurology Q&A pairs while


consuming fewer than 400 GPU hours
                                                     IN
                                            LE
The model’s outputs followed an explicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) framework, dividing the diagnostic process into

                                        C
four steps: symptom analysis, differential diagnosis, recommendations for further examinations, and conclusions. This
                                 TI
                             R
structure made intermediate reasoning transparent and enhanced interpretability. A temperature calibration strategy
                         A
was also applied, and diagnostic outputs were quantified based on attention-weighted uncertainty and misdiagnosis


risk, ultimately providing physicians with diagnostic suggestions that included explicit risk annotations.


Performance evaluation combined automated testing on benchmark tasks—such as MedQA and MedMCQA—with


double-blind scoring of real clinical cases by three senior neurologists. Metrics included misdiagnosis rates, missed


diagnosis rates, and consistency of clinical recommendations. The training and evaluation datasets were strictly


isolated to ensure independence and validity, and all evaluation procedures received ethical approval.


Clinical Study Design


This single-center, prospective study conducted a shadow-mode, non-interventional evaluation of the diagnostic
                                           ARTICLE IN PRESS




performance and recommendation characteristics. The study population comprised consecutive patients who presented


to the Department of Emergency Neurology, Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, between February 1 and


February 14, 2025. The enrolled cases included common neurological emergencies such as cerebrovascular disease,


epilepsy, vertigo, and headache. Patients with emergency neurological conditions were specifically recruited because


their diagnostic processes are highly time-sensitive, carry a high risk of misdiagnosis, and require rapid yet accurate


decision-making. These features made them appropriate for assessing the diagnostic capabilities of an LLM in

                                                                               S
complex emergency settings. All cases were standardized to a uniform format, including consultation audio recordings,
                                                                       ES
physical examination findings, ancillary test results, and demographic data, to ensure input consistency and
                                                               PR
completeness. Xuanwu-NeuroAid and clinicians performed diagnoses independently, and diagnostic accuracy was

                                                      IN
evaluated for both the model and the physicians. The model’s recommendations regarding treatment and examination

                                            LE
were further assessed across three dimensions, with all results blindly scored by senior neurologists.    To explore the

                                        C
influence of sociodemographic factors on the model’s recommendations, each case was constructed in two input
                                  TI
                              R
formats: a conventional version and an extended version that included additional information on educational level and
                         A
type of medical insurance. Differences in the model’s recommendations for further examinations, treatments,


rehabilitation, and health education under these two input conditions were compared. Fig. 1 illustrates the overall


study design.


Study Population


Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) presentation to the Department of Emergency Neurology; (3) availability of


complete case data, including audio recordings of the urgent care visit, physical examination, clinical


laboratory results, and imaging findings; and (4) provision of signed informed consent.


Exclusion criteria: (1) patients admitted directly to the resuscitation pathway due to a critical condition; (2) patients
                                           ARTICLE IN PRESS




with unstable vital signs; (3) patients unable to communicate effectively (e.g., those with severe impairment of


consciousness or marked cognitive dysfunction); (4) patients clearly referred to other departments; (5) patients with


multiple visits, in which only the first encounter was retained; and (6) patients concurrently enrolled in other clinical


trials.


Data Collection


During emergency encounters, the entire patient-physician communication process was recorded using an audio

                                                                               S
device. The recordings were transcribed into text through an intelligent transcription system, independently reviewed
                                                                       ES
and corrected by two researchers, and adjudicated by a third researcher when necessary to ensure transcription
                                                               PR
accuracy and logical integrity. In parallel, the most recent data from patients’ emergency medical records were

                                                      IN
collected, including vital signs, physical examination findings, laboratory results, and neuroimaging reports.

                                            LE
Demographic information—such as age, sex, educational level, and type of medical insurance—was also obtained for

                                        C
statistical analyses. All raw data were rigorously de-identified prior to entry into the research database by removing
                                  TI
                              R
personally identifiable information (e.g., names, identification numbers, and contact details) to ensure complete


anonymization.
                         A
The diagnostic information for each patient was categorized into four sequential stages: (1) the preliminary diagnosis


made by emergency department physicians based on medical history, physical examination, and initial test results; (2)


the independent diagnostic output generated by the model after inputting transcribed text and clinical data; (3) the


physician’s adjusted diagnosis after reviewing the model’s inference process and integrating it with their own clinical


judgment—defined as the AI-assisted diagnosis; and (4) the final confirmed diagnosis established through consensus


discussion by an expert panel. A confirmed diagnosis was defined as a consensus diagnosis reached one month after


the initial visit, based on laboratory and imaging findings obtained during hospitalization or follow-up, and determined
                                            ARTICLE IN PRESS




by an expert panel of four senior neurologists, each with more than 10 years of clinical experience. Patients without


confirmed diagnoses (e.g., those without further examinations after the emergency visit) were excluded from the


diagnostic accuracy analyses. To ensure independence of the study process and objectivity of the results, diagnostic


outputs and recommendations generated by the model were completely isolated from physicians’ initial judgments


until the AI-assisted diagnostic phase. The operators of Xuanwu-NeuroAid had no access to confirmed diagnoses


during model inference, ensuring that the reasoning process remained independent of the reference standard. Similarly,

                                                                                S
members of the expert panel responsible for determining the final confirmed diagnoses were blinded to the model


outputs to minimize the risk of evaluation bias.

Statistical Analysis


A non-inferiority design was adopted for this study, with the assumption that both emergency physicians and the model


would achieve a diagnostic accuracy of 45%. The non-inferiority margin was set at 10%, and with a one-sided


significance level of 0.025, a sample size of 433 participants was required to achieve 80% statistical power. For data
                                          ARTICLE IN PRESS




analysis, McNemar’s test was used to compare the consistency between the independent model diagnoses, physicians’


preliminary diagnoses, and AI-assisted diagnoses relative to the confirmed diagnoses. Likert scale ratings were


analyzed using Friedman’s rank-sum test for repeated measures. When overall differences were significant, pairwise


Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction were performed to adjust for multiple comparisons.


Quantitative scores were assigned to model-generated recommendations for examinations, treatments, rehabilitation,


and health education (examinations: 0–3 points; treatments: 0–3 points; rehabilitation and health education: 0–1 point

                                                                              S
each). McNemar’s test was used to compare differences in the model’s recommendations before and after
                                                                      ES
incorporating demographic information. Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression models were applied to examine
                                                              PR
the interaction effects of educational level, type of medical insurance, and disease category on changes in the model-

                                                     IN
generated recommendations. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically


significant.                                LE
